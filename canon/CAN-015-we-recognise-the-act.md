# We recognise the act; we never buy the game

> **Summary:** Incentives exist to make relations flourish, not to squeeze
> out work. A reward here comes after the act, is read from what was done,
> belongs to whoever earned it, and is judged by the culture people live
> when nobody obliges them.
> **Epistemic:** Why and how does Numinia reward anyone?
> **Pragmatic:** Hold any reward — for the team, for a citizen, for an
> organisation that adopts the game — up to six tests, and refuse the one
> that fails.
> **Audience:** Everyone

**Binds:** whoever designs, grants or audits a reward, a rank, a title or a
recognition in Numinia's name.

---

## What an incentive is for

An incentive is a promise about what happens after you act. Most
organisations use it to get more of something: more hours, more sales, more
tickets closed. We use it for something else. Incentives here exist so that
relations flourish — between the people who work together, and between them
and what they make. A reward that raises the output and sours the table has
failed, however good the numbers look.

That is why the inheritance of the old factory does not serve: it pays for
the hour and punishes the error, and gets exactly what it pays for — people
who count hours and hide errors.

## Six tests

**It feeds three needs and starves none.** People keep doing what leaves them
free to choose (autonomy), what makes them better at something (competence),
and what binds them to others (relatedness). A reward that feeds one of the
three by starving another — a prize for the individual that breaks the team,
a rule that buys obedience with freedom — is not ours.

**It comes after the act, never before the game.** Promise a prize for
playing and people stop playing for the joy of it; take the prize away and
they stop playing at all. So nothing here is offered to make someone play. We
recognise what was done once it is done, and we read it from what the person
did, never from what they say they did — which is also why the higher ranks
are judged by who someone is, and not counted by how much they produced.
Money and recognition do not mix: money pays the work, recognition
celebrates it.

**What you earn is yours.** A rank, a title, a badge or a good belongs to the
person who earned it, and leaves with them. A reward that can be taken back
when someone goes was a loan.

**It is celebrated in public.** Recognition that happens in private is a
transaction. Ours is said aloud, at the table where the others are, because
the point is the bond and not the prize.

**It rewards cathedrals, not prefab homes.** We look for proactive builders
who want a prosperous future for those who come behind, and what moves them
is to be part of something that lasts. So we reward what endures and serves
the next ones — a piece others build on, a rule that saves a question, a
newcomer taught — and never volume for its own sake.

**It is judged by the culture people live, not the one we proclaim.** There
is usually a gap between the culture an organisation proclaims and the one
it lives; ours has to be practised, or it is not ours. The test is what
people do when nobody obliges them: initiatives nobody asked for, time in the
city outside the rituals that are due, contributions nobody commissioned. If
people only take part when they must, the incentive has failed — and it is
the incentive that changes, not the people. We keep the loops that tell us
so, to avoid being completely delusional about our own system.

## Three tables, one rule

The same six tests hold at three tables: the house's own team, human and
digital; the citizens who play in the city; and every organisation that
adopts the game to work better. What each table recognises, and how, is
written where that table lives. None of them may pass a reward that fails a
test here.
