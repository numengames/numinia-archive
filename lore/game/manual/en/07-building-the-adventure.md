# **CHAPTER 7**

# **BUILDING THE ADVENTURE**
**The heartbeat of the board**

«In the half-light of the Agora, a group of citizens gathered around a circle traced in a dust of symbols. There was neither master nor spectator: each of them brought a fragment of story, an object torn from the memory of the city. A broken mask. A question never answered. An unfinished melody.

When they placed them at the centre, the lines on the ground began to spread like luminous veins. Streets, rivers, whole squares rose from that geometry, but none of them was fixed: they quivered, as if awaiting the weight of a decision.

‒Listen‒ murmured the Pythia, her eyes rolled back‒. It is not the world that tells the story. It is the story that gives shape to the world.

The visions intertwined: a port that existed only when someone remembered it, a river that ran backwards, a temple where every mural changed according to who looked at it. They were possibilities, not certainties. Rumours of futures that could be written or could fade away.

The players looked at one another. No one held absolute control. Every gesture, every word added a layer to the nascent weave. A Guardian raised his spear, and the crack in a wall became a door. An Alchemist named a metal that did not exist, and at once a blazing forge appeared at the edge of the square. A Spectral whispered a verse, and the sky answered with an omen.

The die spun upon the stone, but it did not fall in silence: its echo multiplied the options, opening paths that forked beyond imagination.

There was no single director, no predetermined script. Only a chorus of voices that, as they intertwined, invented the impossible. Thus did Numinia rise once more: not out of obedience, but out of shared creation.

For every story begins with a convulsion of fantasy.

For in Numinia adventure is not discovered, but built.»

In a traditional roleplaying game, preparing an adventure module usually consists of establishing in advance a good part of its narrative structure: an initial situation, the events that brought it about, the NPCs involved and their motivations, the places the characters will be able to explore, the conflicts they will meet and the pieces of information that will allow them to understand what has happened. Although the players' decisions may alter the planned route, the GD usually has an underlying plot that they know before the game begins: they know what has happened, why it has happened and what relationships exist between its different elements. The adventure consists, to a large extent, in allowing the PCs to come into contact with that prepared structure and decide how to find their way within it.

And although this structure has brought those of us who love roleplaying enormous rewards for more than thirty years, in Numinia adventures are not built on those premises, as linear sequences or as prewritten plots that the players must follow step by step. The cosmos of the Veil ‒the force that sustains, connects and distorts the reality of the city‒ does not readily admit straight lines or closed structures either: it manifests itself through resonances, correspondences, folds of meaning and relationships that are not always evident. The structure of an adventure must answer to that same nature. The GD does not prepare a story for the players to act out and simply find their way through, but a narrative space capable of producing different stories depending on what happens during the game.

This conception draws on an essential influence from the system _Carved from Brindlewood_ (CfB), by Jason Cordova, and especially from its proposal of «emergent mystery». In CfB, a mystery does not necessarily contain a true solution that the GD knows in advance and that the players must correctly reconstruct. The investigation provides clues and relationships from which the players themselves work out a coherent explanation. The truth, therefore, does not remain hidden behind the adventure waiting to be discovered: it emerges during play. It is a puzzle not yet assembled, one that admits different ways of being put together.

Numinia adopts this principle, but broadens its scope. An adventure need not be articulated around a mystery, but around a conflict understood in its broadest sense: an inexplicable disappearance, a dispute between factions, an anomaly of the Veil, an expedition, a threat, a political event, a social transformation, a creature, an intimate problem or any situation capable of setting the PCs in motion. For this reason, Numinia does not work exclusively with clues, but with Pieces of Conflict: pieces of information, clues, events, images, testimonies, symbols, objects, contradictions or any other meaningful element that can be incorporated into the interpretation of what is happening. The preceding template already establishes this function of the Pieces as elements that the players gather and later relate to one another in order to build an overall interpretation.

The starting point of that process is called the Root. The Root is the fundamental proposition of the adventure: what is happening when play begins and what justifies the characters' intervention. But it does not contain the definitive explanation of what has happened, nor does it prescribe where the story must go. A good Root establishes a situation charged with potential and with the capacity to generate possibilities without determining which of them will end up being developed. It is, in this sense, an essentially semiotic structure: it proposes signs, tensions and relationships meaningful enough to admit multiple interpretations and multiple drifts.

That is why building a Numinia adventure does not consist of developing in advance a causal chain of the kind _this has happened for this reason, the characters will discover that and finally they will have to do this other thing_. Doing so would mean deciding the story before the players have been able to take part in its creation. The Root must be firm in what it presents and extraordinarily flexible in what it means. The GD knows the initial situation, its elements, its forces and its possibilities; they do not yet need to know the story that will explain all of it.

That story will be built during the game. The players will continually contribute new elements through their decisions, their questions, their interpretations and the various mechanics of collaborative creation. A Heterocosmic Roll can introduce an object, a bond or a property that did not exist until then; the system expressly establishes that these creations come to shape the adventure and that Numinia reacts to them. The Functions of the Tale can make unexpected events emerge; collective creation can alter the course of the journey; a question asked by the GD can lead a player to define an aspect of the context that no one had foreseen. Even a spontaneous contribution made during a conversation can open up an extraordinary possibility.

No coherent contribution should fall into the void. This does not mean that every idea voiced by a player immediately becomes an absolute truth, nor that all of them must carry identical weight. It means that the GD listens to contributions as narrative material and determines what resonance they acquire within the adventure. One idea may simply become a detail of the setting; another may reappear several scenes later as a cross-cutting storyline; a third may transform a Piece of Conflict, introduce a new NPC or alter the relationships between two factions. And, occasionally, a contribution will be so fertile and so well connected to what has happened that it will completely change the direction of the adventure, to the point of forcing a reinterpretation of the Root itself.

Therein lies one of the GD's fundamental tasks in Numinia: to integrate without submitting, to guide without imposing. Collaborative creation does not mean that any declaration by the players can arbitrarily modify the world. Every contribution must respect the coherence of Numinia, the information already established and the limits proper to the situation. Creative freedom works within a context, precisely because a context without limits does not generate meaningful possibilities: it generates noise. The GD guards those limits, but deliberately leaves open whatever has not yet been defined.

This form of collaborative creation requires the player to maintain a flexible relationship with their own role during the game. At certain moments they will act strictly from the perspective of their PC: they will decide what the PC does, says, interprets or how they react to what they perceive. At others, they will have to leave that perspective momentarily and observe the scene as a player, attending to the narrative context from outside the character. The questions of the dynamic we know as Dialogic Resonance make this shift especially visible: when the GD asks «what tells you that this place is dangerous?» or «what object is that delegate absent-mindedly fiddling with?», they are not necessarily asking what the PC knows, but inviting the player to complete a still-open part of the fiction. Once the contribution has been made, it is incorporated into the context and the player returns to their character's gaze, and the character may relate to it as to any other element of the world. Stepping in and out of the PC intermittently is not, therefore, a break in the roleplaying, but one of the conditions of shared authorship in Numinia: while the player inhabits the world as a PC, they help to give it shape from their personal position.

This way of playing demands a different kind of preparation. The GD prepares elements, relationships, possibilities and tensions, but avoids preparing conclusions. They design NPCs with interests, places open to exploration, Pieces of Conflict capable of taking on different meanings and events that can be set in motion when the fiction needs them. They then organise these materials by means of Phases, large narrative blocks that make it possible to control the pace and function of each moment of the adventure without determining the specific scenes that must take place. Phases are states of the narration, not chapters of a previously written plot: they serve to order creative chaos without taming it. This modular function already constitutes the central principle of the Numinia Adventure Template.

In this way, the adventure arises from the dialogue between the prepared and the emergent. The GD introduces the Root; the players observe it, question it and begin to modify it; the Pieces of Conflict provide material with which to interpret it; the narrative mechanics introduce new alterations; the Phases organise the resulting movement; and the PCs' decisions determine which possibilities gain importance and which are left behind. The final result does not belong entirely to the GD or to the players. It belongs to the relationship that has been established between them during play.

The resources we present in this chapter are the tools needed to direct that process. In the coming sections we shall see how to build a sufficiently fertile Root, how to devise and combine the Phases, how to manage the Pieces of Conflict, how to offer information without predetermining interpretations, how to use Rumourology and questions about the context, how to encourage collaborative creation and, no less important, how to set its limits. Because directing Numinia does not consist of knowing in advance what story is going to be told.

It consists of carefully preparing the conditions for one to appear that no one had foreseen.

## **Fragment 1: Creating the Root**

Every Numinia adventure begins with a Root. The Root is the fundamental proposition on which the module is built: it establishes what is happening when the adventure begins, why that event requires the PCs' intervention and what initial tension sets the story in motion. It is not a summary of the plot, nor does it contain a secret explanation of the events. It is a situation defined enough to be played from and open enough to admit developments that the GD does not yet know.

This difference is essential. In a conventional adventure, the GD might prepare the premise that several citizens have disappeared because a clandestine organisation is experimenting on them in an abandoned facility, and then distribute the clues needed for the players to discover that truth. In Numinia, that formulation contains too much. If the GD already knows who is causing the disappearances, where the missing are, what the culprit hopes to achieve and what the causal relationship is between all those elements, they have not built a Root: they have built a solution. The players' participation may modify later events, but a fundamental part of the adventure's meaning will already have been decided before it begins.

The Root must stop sooner. It might establish, for example, that citizens are disappearing in the Vitruvian District and that, several days later, some of their personal belongings reappear arranged in public places following an apparently deliberate pattern. That is something that is happening. It can be investigated, observed and interpreted, but it does not yet explain anything. Have the citizens been kidnapped? Have they disappeared of their own accord? Do the objects constitute a message? Is someone placing them there? Is there an anomaly of the Veil? Is there some relationship between the missing? The Root does not answer these questions because the adventure does not yet need those answers. It needs to produce the conditions for them to appear.

### **The Root establishes facts, not explanations**

To build a Root, the GD must distinguish between founding facts and open interpretations. Founding facts are those circumstances that must be true for the adventure to exist: something has disappeared, a faction asks for help, an anomaly is producing a certain phenomenon, two institutions are locked in conflict, an expedition has not returned or an impossible object has appeared where it should not be. These facts constitute the firm ground on which the game will begin and need not remain in doubt.

What must remain open is the meaning of those facts and the deep relationships that exist between them. Who is responsible, why it is happening, what a particular figure is after, what relationship exists between two events, what the true nature of an anomaly is or what consequences intervening in it will have are questions that, whenever they constitute fertile material for the adventure, should not be settled in the Root. The Pieces of Conflict, the PCs' decisions and the various forms of collaborative creation will later provide materials with which to build those relationships. The system already allows for the Pieces to be clues, symbols, events or anomalies, and for the players to later articulate them into a coherent interpretation.

Therefore, a Root does not have to be vague in order to be open. In fact, exactly the opposite should be the case. _«Something strange is happening in the Ouroboros District»_ offers very little to play with. We do not know who is affected, what is happening or what makes the situation extraordinary. By contrast, _«for the past seven nights, everyone who walks down a certain street in the Old Quarter dreams of exactly the same room, although each of them remembers that there was a different object inside»_ is much more concrete and, at the same time, much more open. It defines the phenomenon precisely and leaves its meaning intact.

The openness of a Root does not depend on the amount of information it leaves out, but on the number of interpretations that the information it contains allows.

### **A tension that demands movement**

Nor should the Root merely present a curiosity. It must contain an active tension, something that makes it reasonable for the PCs to intervene and that allows the situation to evolve if they do not. A statue that has begun to change colour may be intriguing; a statue that changes colour every time a name disappears from the civil registers already contains a relationship capable of setting the adventure in motion. The tension may be dangerous, political, intimate, social, metaphysical or even seemingly trivial, but it must introduce some form of imbalance.

This does not mean that the GD must prepare what will happen if the players do not follow a certain path. It is enough for the Root to contain forces at work. NPCs want things, institutions react, phenomena go on occurring and conflicts generate consequences.

The players do not arrive at a motionless stage set that patiently awaits their investigation; they arrive at a situation that was already under way before they appeared and that will go on transforming after their arrival.

### **The test of questions**

There is a simple way to check whether a Root is open enough: ask questions about it. A good Root should immediately provoke several relevant questions whose answers the GD has not yet decided. It is not a matter of ignoring information needed to direct the game, but of identifying those aspects whose discovery may legitimately belong to play.

Let us take the disappearance of citizens whose belongings reappear arranged in public spaces. The Root generates questions on its own: what relationship exists between the missing? Who places the objects? What does their arrangement mean? Why do precisely those objects reappear? Where are their owners? If the GD can answer all of them in advance and those answers form a coherent causal explanation, they have probably gone too far: behind the Root, once again, a plot lies hidden waiting to be discovered. If, on the other hand, the situation does not provoke any concrete question, the Root is probably too vague to generate adventure.

There is, therefore, an especially useful test: the GD must know clearly what is happening, but not necessarily what it means.

### **The Root as a generative structure**

The function of the Root is not to remain intact throughout the whole adventure, but to generate fiction. From it will spring the first scenes, the Pieces of Conflict, the Dialogic Resonances and the characters' possibilities for action. Some relationships will appear because the module has prepared them; others will be born from the PCs' actions, from a Heterocosmic Roll, from an unexpected interpretation or from an especially fertile answer to a Resonance. Numinia's modular structure exists precisely to allow those materials to alter the course without «breaking» the adventure.

This implies that the Root possesses factual stability but interpretive plasticity. If the adventure begins because nine citizens have disappeared, it is not advisable to decide arbitrarily halfway through the session that they never disappeared simply because a more attractive idea has come up. What has happened is already part of the shared world. What can indeed be radically transformed is our understanding of those disappearances. A contribution from the players may turn them into something the GD had never imagined when writing the module, provided that the new interpretation is compatible with the established facts and finds sufficient support in what has happened during the game.

A moment may even come when the accumulated contributions force a reinterpretation of the Root itself. This does not mean that the adventure has escaped the GD's control. It means that the Root has fulfilled its function correctly: instead of leading the players towards a predetermined story, it has produced enough narrative material for a new story to appear.

### **What a Root should contain**

A Root should be able to answer four questions clearly:

1. What is happening? The concrete facts that make up the initial situation.

2. Where does it happen, and whom does it affect? The context of Numinia on which the conflict acts.

3. What tension does it introduce? Whatever turns the situation into a problem, anomaly or imbalance capable of generating adventure.

4. Why can the PCs intervene? The circumstance that brings them into contact with the conflict, without yet determining what they will have to do with it.

And it should avoid answering four others in advance:

1. What is the true explanation of everything that has happened?

2. Who is necessarily responsible?

3. What interpretation must the players reach?

4. How must the conflict be resolved?

The difference between the two lists is practically the difference between preparing a situation and writing a plot.

A well-built Root is not, therefore, the first stretch of a road whose destination the GD knows. It is a system of coordinates from which several paths may set out, including some that did not exist when the session began. It must contain enough substance to withstand the players' questions and enough indeterminacy to surprise even the one who wrote it.

The GD plants the Root.

The table discovers what kind of tree was growing beneath it.

### **Founding facts and potential proposals**

The Root may contain something more than the founding facts of the Starting Conflict. The GD may also prepare NPCs, places, events, objects, relationships or images that bear some possible connection to the conflict and that may be incorporated into the adventure if they find the right moment. These potential proposals do not constitute facts of the fiction until they appear during play: they are materials available for the GD to introduce, transform or discard according to the direction the adventure takes.

Nor should a potential proposal be completely defined. In fact, it tends to be more fertile when it combines, as will be better explained in the next section, on Dialogic Resonance, _arbitrary elements_ and _open elements_. The GD may establish that there is a Game Master who knows something important about the disappearances, without yet deciding what exactly they know, why they know it, what their intentions are or what position they hold with respect to the Starting Conflict. If they do finally appear, some of those elements may be established directly and others may become new Spaces of Indeterminacy: _«The Game Master receives you as though they had been waiting for you for several days. What detail of the room makes you think they knew exactly when you would arrive?»_. A proposal prepared by the GD thus becomes material for a new intervention by the players.

This makes it possible to prepare a rich Root without turning it into a covert plot. The GD may imagine numerous possibilities around the Starting Conflict: characters who might intervene, places to which the investigation might lead, events liable to be precipitated, significant objects or relationships still uncertain. Preparing a possibility does not oblige one to turn it into an event. Its function is to provide the GD with materials with which to respond nimbly to the development of the fiction, not to establish a list of contents the adventure must work through.

And, without doubt, all these proposals must be prepared to promote narrative, not to offer answers.

### **Learning to let go**

Directing an adventure built in this way demands a particular kind of flexibility: the GD must be willing to abandon their own proposals. During preparation, magnificent ideas will appear, promising characters, tempting revelations and relationships that seem destined to produce memorable scenes. Some will reach the table. Many others will not. As the players incorporate their own proposals, establish relationships between the Pieces of Conflict and, through their decisions, transform the direction of the adventure, part of the prepared material will cease to fit the fiction that is emerging. When this happens, the GD should not force its appearance merely because it had been planned.

This renunciation is an essential part of collaborative creation. What is prepared belongs to the GD; what is played belongs to the table. As long as a proposal remains only in their notes, it does not yet constitute a truth about Numinia. An NPC who never appeared did not secretly take part in the events; a conspiracy that never came to be incorporated into the fiction was not happening behind the scenes; a revelation that was prepared but never spoken is not a truth the players failed to discover. They are possibilities that never came to materialise, and they can vanish without leaving any narrative debt.

The criterion for keeping a proposal should not be how much enthusiasm it aroused during preparation, but how much sense it retains within the adventure that is actually taking place. If the players' contributions open a more fertile direction, some of the GD's ideas can be adapted to it; others will have to be profoundly modified, and others, quite simply, abandoned. Bringing them back by force would mean privileging a private fiction over the shared fiction. The GD does not direct in order to prove that their forecasts were good: they direct in order to recognise which of all the available possibilities are producing play.

This does not make wasted preparation lost time. A discarded proposal has already fulfilled a function by allowing the GD to understand the possibilities of the Root and to arrive at the table with enough materials to react. They may even keep it for another adventure, provided they do not try to slip it surreptitiously into a place where it no longer belongs. The preparation of a Numinia adventure must be abundant in possibilities and austere in commitments.

That is why a good Root may contain much more than will finally appear. Its richness is not measured by the quantity of content the players manage to discover, but by the number of directions it makes possible. The Starting Conflict will remain the core for as long as the fiction sustains it; around it, characters, events, relationships and meanings may appear, transform or disappear as the adventure finds its own shape.

And the GD will have to acquire a virtue that is difficult for any creator: learning to live with their unrevealed secrets.

## **Fragment 2: Dialogic Resonance**

Dialogic Resonance is a dynamic of collaborative creation through which the GD puts to one or more players a directed question about some element of the fiction whose meaning or manifestation still remains open. The question always starts from a previously established context and contains a premise concrete enough to guide the answer, but leaves one of its aspects undetermined so that it is the players who complete it.

Thus, the GD does not ask _«what is in the Ministerial Hall?»_, but _«what tells you that danger can be sensed in the Ministerial Hall?»_. The existence of the Hall, the presence of the PCs and the sense of danger already belong to the fiction; whatever manifests that danger remains open. The player's answer completes that space of indeterminacy and becomes part of the shared context.

The Resonance is dialogic because meaning comes neither exclusively from the GD nor from the player, but from the relationship between the two: the GD delimits a field of possibilities through the question, and the player introduces an interpretation that modifies the fiction. That new reality may afterwards be taken up, reinterpreted or developed by any of the participants, so that the narration advances through a succession of shared meanings and not through the revelation of previously established contents.

### **The Space of Indeterminacy**

The question that activates a Dialogic Resonance opens what we shall call a Space of Indeterminacy: a part of the fiction that the module and the GD deliberately leave unspecified so that it can be completed during play. However, this is not an empty space for the player to fill as they please ‒if we set out to play with empty spaces so that our freedom is not curtailed, it is too late when we discover that the void is, by definition, a barren territory in which nothing bears fruit, for there is nothing in it to play with, and that is tragic‒. The very question of the Dialogic Resonance, then, already contains a proposal: it establishes certain conditions, relationships or meanings and leaves open only that which can manifest them. In _«what tells you that danger can be sensed in the Ministerial Hall?»_, for example, the danger already exists as a condition of the scene; what is undetermined is its sign. The player might perceive that all the clocks have stopped, notice that the officials speak without looking at one another, or discover a dark stain that someone has tried to hide beneath a rug. The possibilities are countless, but no answer starts from nothing: all of them are born from the field of meaning the question has opened.

This establishes a fundamental difference between indeterminacy and arbitrariness. A question such as _«what is here?»_ can offer so much freedom that, paradoxically, it gives the player hardly anything to work with. Dialogic Resonance proceeds the other way round: the more suggestive the premise contained in the question, the greater the number of meaningful paths that can arise from it.

The GD does not decide the answer, but does prepare the ground on which it can grow. A good Dialogic Resonance therefore establishes fertile limits: it determines enough to stimulate the imagination and leaves enough undetermined for the player's contribution to be truly their own, authentically free.

### **The rule as a field of possibilities**

This principle expresses a conception of the rules of any game that, moreover, runs through Numinia in its entirety: a rule does not exist solely to determine what a player cannot do; it exists, above all, to show them within which field they can act. Absolute freedom has no relief: if anything is equally possible at any moment, no decision meets resistance, direction or meaning. No decision has value, then, nor any way of unfolding.

Just as a game needs a board, some pieces or some conditions for play to exist at all, shared creation needs structures capable of turning an unlimited possibility into a set of meaningful possibilities.

The rules thus make us a gift of an unploughed field. But a fertile field. They do not tell the player which furrow to open or what they will find at the end of the land; they do something far more important: they hand them a terrain on which their decisions can leave a mark. Fertile limitation does not reduce creation; it provokes it. A Dialogic Resonance does not say _invent anything_, because that absolute freedom abandons the player before the tragedy of the void; nor does it say _this is exactly what happens_, because then nothing is left to create. It says, instead: _this is happening; tell me how it manifests_. Between arbitrariness and prescription appears the space proper to play.

For this reason, the Space of Indeterminacy must be designed. The GD can introduce it spontaneously during the session, but a module can also point out those places especially fertile for a Dialogic Resonance: a gesture whose meaning is not specified, an anomaly whose appearance remains open, a relationship that still lacks a cause, an incomplete image or a circumstance of which we know only the consequences. They are not omissions of the module. They are places the module has decided not to close because another voice will have to intervene to complete them.

The adventure is not simply unfinished: it is prepared to be finished by playing.

### **The answer as proposal**

When a player answers a Dialogic Resonance, their answer immediately ceases to be a possibility and becomes a proposal incorporated into the fiction. This distinction is essential. The GD does not ask a question in order to hear ideas they can afterwards ignore while carrying on with the adventure they had planned. If they have deliberately ceded a Space of Indeterminacy, they take on the commitment to accept whatever the players introduce into it, provided it respects the limits set by the question itself and does not contradict elements already consolidated in the world or the adventure. Collaborative creation can only exist if the participants trust that what they contribute has consequences.

There is, therefore, a responsibility of the GD towards the player's proposal. It does not consist in always granting them the interpretation that most benefits them, nor in giving each answer an outsized importance. It consists in recognising it, incorporating it and allowing it to find its place in the fiction. An answer can be small without being irrelevant. The object the Cyanite delegate was fiddling with may never again decide the course of the conflict, but from that instant that object exists, characterises the delegate and forms part of the shared memory of the scene. And perhaps three Phases later someone will remember that he was turning a tiny bone key between his fingers. Good stories have the dreadful habit of remembering good details.

The magnitude of that incorporation will depend on the narrative power of the proposal and on the relationships it establishes with the rest of the adventure. Some answers will remain as meaningful details, enriching a place, a character or a situation. Others will find later resonances and become transversal stories that run through several Phases without necessarily displacing the main conflict. And some, through their capacity to connect Pieces of Conflict, shed light on earlier events or open especially fertile possibilities, may become main paths of the adventure, to the point of altering its direction or transforming the interpretation of the Root itself.

The GD need not decide immediately to which of these categories a contribution belongs: they can let it breathe and observe what relationships it produces during play.

This also means that integrating is not the same as rewarding the most spectacular notion. A simple contribution can acquire enormous importance if it finds fertile relationships with what happens afterwards; an extraordinarily showy idea may remain simply a magnificent detail if it establishes no other connection. The GD does not judge answers by their extravagance, but listens for their capacity to produce meaning. Numinia already establishes that the players' creation modifies the adventure and that the world reacts to what they introduce; Dialogic Resonance extends that same principle to the ordinary construction of context.

In this way a small pact is established between GD and players: the GD offers a structure that deserves to be interpreted, and the players respond with contributions that deserve to be taken seriously. Neither party possesses the fiction on its own. One proposes the conditions; the others explore their possibilities; and whatever arises from the encounter modifies the conditions that follow.

Dialogic Resonance does not ask the player what they want to exist.

It asks them what they can discover within that which we have not yet decided.

And I believe this last opposition is particularly important. It prevents the mechanic from being perceived as a capricious surrender of narrative authority. The player does not receive the GD's hat for a few seconds: they receive a space designed for their authorship.

That difference, small in appearance, is practically the whole philosophy of the mechanic.

### **How to formulate a Dialogic Resonance**

A Dialogic Resonance is built by combining an _arbitrary element_ and an _open element_. The arbitrary element is a condition the GD introduces as true and which the player must not decide: it gives the question direction and delimits the field of creation. The open element is whatever the question deliberately leaves undetermined and which it falls to the player to specify. The simplest formula is, therefore: «This is so; what makes it so?», although it can take many other forms. In _«what tells you that danger can be sensed in the Ministerial Hall?»_, the fact that there is some manifestation of danger is the arbitrary element; what that manifestation is constitutes the open element. In _«what is the Cyanite delegate fiddling with in his hand?»_, the GD arbitrarily establishes that the delegate is absent-mindedly handling some object, but leaves it to the player to decide which one. Arbitrary does not mean capricious or unjustified here: it simply means withheld from the player's decision in order to provide a concrete point from which to create.

The proportion between the two elements determines the quality of the question. If the arbitrary element is missing, the void appears; if the open element is missing, creation disappears. Asking _«what is in this room?»_ offers a field so unlimited that it provides hardly any materials to think with; asking _«why is there a bloodstained key on the table?»_ has already determined the object, its state and its relevance, and only allows a rather narrow causal relationship to be completed. A good Resonance must sit between the two extremes: it affirms something specific enough to awaken associations, but leaves open a variable broad enough to admit genuinely different answers. The GD can check this very simply before formulating it: they must be able to point out precisely what they are affirming and what they are handing over to the player. If they cannot tell the two apart, the question is probably not yet well built.

Before formulating a Dialogic Resonance, the GD must be able to answer two questions: «What am I establishing?» and «What am I letting the player establish?».

### **Resources for enriching a Resonance**

The structure of a Dialogic Resonance is always the same, but what it asks about can come from very different places. The GD need not confine themselves to filling in the immediate context of a scene: they can use the question to establish relationships with the PCs, recover earlier elements of the adventure or set two seemingly unrelated aspects of the fiction in dialogue with each other. The following resources are not additional rules, but suggestions that may prove useful: ways of choosing the arbitrary and open elements of the question with greater intention.

**Introducing the PC's intimacy.** The question can give some element of the setting a personal relationship with one of the characters: _«What object from your childhood do you find in the filing cabinet?»_, _«What is there in this woman's voice that reminds you of someone you once knew?»_. The player does not merely complete the world, but introduces something of their PC's biography or sensibility. It is best to use this resource on aspects that have not yet been established: the Resonance may widen the character's intimacy, but it should not rewrite it.

**Recovering what has already been played.** A question can force a look back and give a new function to something that appeared earlier: _«What does the manual explain about the use of the anemochronous clocks that appeared during the Presentation Phase?»_, _«What detail of this symbol do you recognise from the one you saw in the antiquarian's house?»_. This resource is especially fertile because it turns continuity into creation: an element that seemed incidental can retrospectively acquire an unexpected importance.

**Relating two distant elements.** The GD can place in the same question two aspects of the adventure that until then had no relationship: _«What detail of the guards' uniform reminds you of the artefact you found at the Ascending Falls of Kirelya?»_. The player determines the link, but the question proposes that some link exists. It is an especially powerful tool for producing emergent connections between Pieces of Conflict.

**Asking about a consequence, not a cause.** Instead of asking the player to explain why something happens, they can first be shown its effect: _«What has stopped working in the square since that light appeared?»_, _«What do the inhabitants of the neighbourhood do to avoid passing in front of that building?»_. Consequences are usually easier to imagine than explanations and, moreover, they preserve the mystery: we know that something is happening, but we do not yet know why.

**Asking about the clue, not the truth.** If the adventure contains an important uncertainty, it is preferable to ask about what allows it to be interpreted rather than about its definitive explanation. _«What do you find in the office that makes you suspect the minister was expecting the attack?»_ is more fertile than _«why did the minister know they were going to attack him?»_. The first introduces a new Piece of Conflict; the second runs the risk of resolving what ought to remain open for now.

**Introducing an anomaly.** The arbitrary element can consist in stating that something does not fit and leaving the nature of that irregularity open: _«There is something impossible about the layout of these rooms. What is it?»_, _«One of the portraits should not be here. What does it depict?»_. The anomaly immediately provides direction and interest without determining its meaning.

**Giving the context a voice.** Not every contribution has to take material form in objects or events. A Resonance can ask about customs, prejudices, rumours, superstitions or collective interpretations: _«What do the inhabitants of the Old Quarter say must never be done after midnight in this street?»_. In this way, the players can help to build not only the physical space of Numinia, but also the culture through which its inhabitants interpret it.

And we shall make one last, particularly important recommendation: ask about what the GD is willing not to control. A Dialogic Resonance should not be used to steer the player surreptitiously towards an answer the GD already has in mind. If they need the delegate to be carrying a key, they should say that the delegate is carrying a key; they should not ask _«what small, toothed metal object capable of opening a lock is the delegate carrying?»_ and then feign exquisite surprise at the answer.

When the GD opens a Space of Indeterminacy, they must do so because they want to discover what will be in it.

This last rule almost deserves to be highlighted, because it perfectly seals the pact we have been hinting at and defining: do not ask what you have already decided the answer to. The Resonance then ceases to be a technique for coaxing «the right answer» out of the player and truly becomes a tool of shared discovery. That is where it begins to become deliciously dangerous.

## **Fragment 3: The Pieces of Conflict**

The Pieces of Conflict are significant elements that appear during the adventure and bear some potential relationship to the Starting Conflict. They can be objects, documents, testimonies, images, symbols or any other material and tangible manifestation capable of acquiring relevance within the fiction. The PCs find, provoke or recognise them as the adventure unfolds and, later on, they will be able to relate them to one another in order to formulate their own interpretations of what is happening.

But a Piece of Conflict is not a clue in the traditional sense. A clue usually points to a truth that already exists: the footprint belongs to the murderer, the letter proves a blackmail, the key opens a particular door. Its function is to allow the players to reconstruct correctly a relationship that the author of the adventure has established beforehand. A Piece works differently. It has relevance without yet having a closed meaning. When it appears, we know that it matters; we do not necessarily know why yet.

That is why the best Pieces have something of the anomaly, the find or the oddity about them. They demand attention. A business card from an establishment that vanished decades ago, a photograph from which someone has been carefully erased, a recording that contains thirteen seconds of silence at the same point every time it is played, or a statue found in a place where it manifestly does not belong are all good material for a Piece. Not because they conceal a solution the GD has decided in advance, but because they seem to demand an explanation.

The Piece provides a frame of meaning, not its resolution. It may contain perfectly determined facts ‒the card belongs to a specific bar; the photograph was taken on a particular date; the recording comes from a certain machine‒, but the relationships that make those facts part of the Conflict remain open. The players thus have something solid to think about without the module having done the thinking for them.

### **Pregenerated Pieces and emergent Pieces**

The GD can include some Pieces of Conflict while preparing the adventure. These pregenerated Pieces form part of the Root's set of possibilities and provide significant material that may appear when the fiction allows them to be introduced. As with potential proposals, preparing them does not oblige one to use them. A Piece that ceases to be pertinent can be transformed, held in reserve or disappear from the adventure without the players having lost anything: what never entered the fiction never became part of it.

But many Pieces will arise during play itself. A decision by the PCs, a Dialogic Resonance, a Heterocosmic Roll, an unexpected conversation or an interpretation by the players can create the conditions for the GD to introduce an element they had never prepared. These emergent Pieces have exactly the same value as the pregenerated ones. Their legitimacy does not depend on having been written before the session began, but on their arising coherently from what is happening.

Let us imagine that the PCs are questioning an agent of the Summa Archive. During the scene, one of the players decides that their PC will try to steal his wallet. Nothing in the preparation contemplated this possibility, but the action succeeds. The GD could simply list a few trivial belongings; they can also recognise that the player has just opened a new Space of Indeterminacy. Among the objects there appears a card from an old café in the Corridor of Memory, closed for years, in front of whose façade the first of the individuals under investigation disappeared. The card has just become a Piece of Conflict.

Note where it comes from: the player has produced the opportunity; the GD has responded with a proposal. The Piece thus also works as a narrative reward. The player's initiative does not merely earn an immediate advantage: it makes the world reveal a new surface on which to act.

And, significantly, the GD does not yet need to know why the agent has that card.

### **A Piece should open more than it closes**

A good Piece of Conflict does not merely provide information: it produces new possibilities of play. The café card establishes several things, but it immediately opens as many others. What will the group find if they visit the abandoned establishment? Why does the agent keep that card? What relationship is there between the café and the first disappearance? Does the agent know of that relationship? Is he investigating it, taking part in it, or is he caught up in something he does not understand either?

None of these questions needs an answer at the moment the Piece appears. If the players decide to investigate them, the answers can emerge through new scenes, Resonances, actions by the PCs and new Pieces of Conflict. A Piece can engender other Pieces. Investigation thus does not consist in following a chain of clues designed in advance, but in expanding a network of relationships whose meaning is built up as play goes on.

This gives the GD a working criterion for recognising a fertile Piece: after introducing it, they should be able to formulate several interesting questions about it that they do not yet know how to answer. If the object merely confirms something the GD had already decided, it is probably functioning as a traditional clue. If, on the contrary, its appearance opens up relationships, suspicions and possible lines of action, it is doing the proper work of a Piece of Conflict.

The Pieces, therefore, do not form a chain:

Piece → Piece → Piece → Solution

They form a constellation. Each new element can relate to several earlier ones, alter the interpretation of what was already known or make relevant something that until then seemed secondary. The more Pieces the players gather, the more they have not only information at their disposal, but more possible relationships with which to build meaning.

### **Investigating until there is enough world**

The Pieces also have a mechanical function. The players need to gather at least three Pieces of Conflict before they can leave the Investigation Phase and move on to the Action Phase. The minimum guarantees that the adventure has produced enough significant material so that the players do not have to act upon a practically empty conflict. Three Pieces already make it possible to compare, relate, suspect and begin to formulate an interpretation.

However, three is a threshold, not a goal. The players can keep investigating and gather more Pieces before moving on. Doing so widens the available constellation of meanings and will later have mechanical consequences: the more pertinent Pieces they have managed to gather and articulate, the better their chances in the Prism Rolls of the Resolution Phase. Investigation thus presents a genuine decision. The players can act as soon as they have enough material to do so, or keep exploring the Conflict in search of new relationships that will later strengthen their interpretation.

The GD, for their part, should not turn this possibility into an invitation to accumulate Pieces indefinitely. The fiction must keep breathing: events evolve, NPCs act and the Conflict may transform. Investigating further provides possibilities, but it also means remaining longer inside a situation that will not necessarily wait motionless.

### **The Direction Piece**

Among the Pieces of Conflict there must be at least one that fulfils a particular function: providing the players with a concrete direction from which they can tackle the Starting Conflict directly. We could call it the Direction Piece. Unlike the others, its practical function must be unmistakable, even if its meaning remains open.

Let us return to the abandoned café. The PCs decide to investigate it and find in the back room an old computer containing an encrypted file. After managing to translate it, they discover that it is a report of the Summa Archive recording several waves of disappearances that took place over numerous tetrads. The document repeatedly mentions a secret laboratory located somewhere on the Hesiode Moor and establishes that there is a deep relationship between that place and the disappearances.

The direction is unmistakable: there is something on the Hesiode Moor that deserves to be investigated directly. The players now have a concrete path towards the Action Phase.

But the interpretation remains open.

The report does not necessarily establish that the Summa Archive is responsible for the disappearances. Nor does it state that the victims have been taken to the laboratory, that they are being experimented on there, or even that they are still alive. Perhaps the Archive was investigating the phenomenon; perhaps it was trying to contain it; perhaps someone forged the document; perhaps the laboratory does not receive the disappeared, but something they leave behind when they disappear. The Piece closes the uncertainty about where the group can act, but it does not close the uncertainty about what the thing they will find means.

This distinction is fundamental. The Direction Piece is not «the right clue», nor does it reveal the solution to the conflict. It is a door visible enough for the players to be able to decide to go through it.

### **When to offer a direction**

Nor should the Direction Piece appear in obedience to a rigid counter. There is no special virtue in introducing it as exactly the third, fourth or fifth Piece. The GD must observe, all together, the pace of the narration, the number of Pieces gathered and the fertility of the relationships the players are building.

If the players have barely begun to understand the Conflict, introducing an unmistakable direction too early can rush the adventure before it has produced enough narrative material. If, on the contrary, they have spent a long time accumulating elements without finding any concrete possibility of intervention, artificially prolonging the Investigation can turn openness into paralysis.

There is one particularly useful indicator: the Direction Piece should appear when the players already have things to think about, but need a place ‒material, social, political, symbolic‒ on which to act.

Sometimes it will be a pregenerated Piece that the GD finds the right moment to introduce. At other times it will arise emergently, precisely because one of the players' actions has created a direction much more interesting than the one prepared. Like any other proposal of the GD, the planned Direction Piece can be abandoned if the fiction has produced a better one.

Once they have obtained it, the players decide what to do with it. They may consider that they have enough elements and move on to the Action Phase, or keep investigating to gather new Pieces, test their suspicions and widen the constellation before confronting the Conflict directly. The direction exists; the urgency of following it belongs to the players.

### **The principle of the Pieces**

The Pieces of Conflict make it possible, in short, to replace an architecture of discovery with an architecture of meaning-making. The GD does not hand out fragments of a broken truth in the hope that the players will reassemble it correctly. They introduce significant objects, strange events, incomplete documents, suspicious behaviour and potential relationships that lend resistance and matter to the imagination.

That is why a Piece must satisfy three conditions:

1. It must be concrete. Something has really appeared in the fiction and the players can relate to it.

2. It must be relevant. The way it is presented makes it possible to understand that it bears some potential relationship to the Starting Conflict.

3. It must be open. The exact nature of that relationship is not decided beforehand and admits different interpretations.

If concreteness is lacking, the Piece is an abstraction. If relevance is lacking, it is mere decoration. If openness is lacking, it is a clue.

And the Pieces of Conflict are not there to prove that the GD knew the answer. They are there so that, when an answer finally appears, it seems to have been waiting the whole adventure to be discovered.

## **Fragment 4: Rumourology**

In Numinia, knowledge rarely takes the reassuring form of a definitive truth. The city keeps archives, institutions, libraries and specialists devoted to preserving and organising knowledge, but it is also a cultural organism subject to memory, interpretation and the alterations of the Veil. One and the same event can survive as a document for some, a warning for others and an absurd legend for those who arrived late to the conversation.

Among these strata circulates Rumourology: the body of tales, fragmentary memories, testimonies, hypotheses, superstitions and symbolic associations through which the inhabitants of Numinia try to explain what they know only incompletely.

Rumours do not constitute systematic knowledge. Nor are they necessarily false. A rumour can preserve the distorted shape of a true fact, mix up different events, express the interests of those who pass it on or offer a mistaken interpretation of something that really happened. Others are false from beginning to end and, as befits good falsehoods, can prove extraordinarily informative about those who chose to believe them.

Rumourology is, therefore, the informal memory of Numinia: what the city believes it remembers when nobody can produce a document convincing enough to end the argument.

### **Rumourology as an adventure resource**

Rumourology is an optional resource for building adventures. Not every adventure needs to use it, nor should the GD turn every anomaly into the object of rumours. It is especially fertile when an element has become important enough that it is worth discovering not only _what it is_, but what Numinia thinks about it.

Unlike the Dialogic Resonance, Rumourology is not primarily a tool of collaborative creation. The players can bring it into play through their investigations and decide which elements they wish to look into, but it falls fundamentally to the GD to provide the rumours. Its purpose is to broaden the cultural context of the adventure, introduce new relationships and offer material that the players can accept, suspect, compare, reinterpret or turn into new lines of investigation.

A rumour can be pregenerated in the Root, prepared as a possibility that the GD will introduce if they find an appropriate moment. Like any other element of the Root, it is not immutable: it can be modified to incorporate events and proposals that have arisen during the game.

It can also be emergent. If the players attach importance to an element the GD had never foreseen, the GD can create on the spot one or several rumours expressing how that element has been perceived by different sectors of Numinia. The city thus acquires memory around what the players have decided to make significant.

### **From detail to cultural object**

Not every element of the fiction possesses Rumourology. For something to be investigated in this way it must have become culturised: it must have stopped functioning as an isolated detail and acquired a recognisable presence within the imaginary of Numinia.

Culturisation may come about because an element appears repeatedly, because different characters seem to know of it, because its presence is discovered in different places, or because the PCs themselves establish a sufficiently meaningful relationship with it. At that moment it ceases to be merely _something that has happened in this scene_ and becomes _something about which Numinia may have something to say_.

Let us imagine that a PC notices, in a room of the Prytaneum, a generative abstract painting whose forms produce in them a strange feeling of familiarity. The fact, in itself, may be no more than a detail. Later, in the Hyperborean Tavern, they discover that one of the customers wears a shirt printed with an extraordinarily similar pattern. The repetition establishes a relationship. The motif has begun to become culturised.

The PCs could then investigate its Rumourology and discover, for example:

_«The patterns first appeared several tetrads ago in the dreams of some Oneiromancers of the Solomon District.»_

Or perhaps:

_«There is a superstition that whoever reproduces one of those drawings from memory ends up adding a figure that was not in the original.»_

Neither of the two rumours explains what the patterns really are. Both do something more useful for the adventure: they grant them a past, relationships and possibilities.

### **Investigating Rumourology**

When the players want to find out what is said about a culturised element, they must explain how they gain access to that stratum of collective memory. They may question inhabitants connected with the matter, consult archives and publications, look through private collections, turn to cultural institutions, explore places linked to the phenomenon, or resort to any procedure consistent with the fiction.

Not all sources provide the same kind of rumours. An archive may preserve old versions; a tavern, tales deformed by decades of oral transmission; an institution, official interpretations; a clandestine market, self-interested versions that nobody would admit to in public. Choosing where to ask is therefore part of the investigation.

In game terms, investigating Rumourology is resolved by means of an Inquire Roll, in accordance with the rules for Action Rolls. The GD determines how many rumours the source can provide according to its richness and relevance. Questioning an NPC tangentially connected with the matter may yield a single rumour; consulting a relevant documentary collection, two; gaining access to an especially rich archive could provide several.

The number should not become a rigid tariff. The fundamental question is much simpler: how many interesting versions can this source reasonably contain?

### **Results of the Roll**

The degree obtained in the Inquire Roll determines not only access to the Rumourology but also how much context the PC obtains with which to interpret it.

**Critical Failure**
The PC obtains no relevant rumour. The investigation does not grant access to any significant cultural information about the element being investigated.

**Failure**
The PC obtains an unclassified rumour. They discover what is said, but lack sufficient elements to assess the origin or plausibility of that version.

**Success**
The PC obtains a classified rumour. As well as learning it, they receive an indication of its possible truth or falsehood.

**Total Success**
The PC obtains a classified and commented rumour. As well as roughly assessing its reliability, they gain access to information about its origin, deformation, context or relationship with other elements.

The gradation is important: a better result does not automatically turn the rumour into truth; it allows a better understanding of what kind of rumour it is. Rumourology studies the circulation of knowledge; it does not grant omniscience. Numinia would be a far less interesting place if a good roll were enough to put an end to a good doubt.

### **Classifying a rumour**

When the result allows it to be classified, the GD adds a brief indication to guide the players as to its status. The classification is not a certificate of truth, but an assessment of its plausibility, origin or degree of deformation.

Some possible classifications are:

  - Probably true.

  - Partially true, but biased.

  - Based on a real fact, but distorted.

  - Probably false.

  - Unknown origin.

  - Absolutely plausible.

  - Dangerously close to the truth.

  - As hard to prove as to refute.

The GD may create other classifications when the context requires it. In fact, slightly interpretative formulations tend to prove more evocative than a mathematical scale of reliability. «Probably true» informs; «nobody has managed to prove it, although three institutions have tried to erase it» begins to provoke adventures.

### **Commenting on a rumour**

With a Total Success, the rumour also incorporates a comment. This must not reveal the solution of the Conflict, nor definitively explain what the PCs are investigating. Its function is to provide a second level of reading: to explain who upholds that version, where it appeared, why it might have become deformed, or what relationship it bears to other known elements.

For example:

<u>Rumour:</u> _The clocks of the Corridor of Memory stop for a few seconds before someone disappears._

<u>Classification:</u> _Based on a real fact, but distorted._

<u>Comment:</u> _The earliest versions did not speak of all the clocks, but only of the old anemochronic clocks. The generalisation appeared much later._

The comment does not resolve the rumour. It refines it. And, in refining it, it may offer a new direction for the investigation.

### **Building good rumours**

A fruitful rumour should contain something concrete enough to be usable and something uncertain enough to need interpretation. Saying «strange things are told about that building» provides scarcely any game material. Saying «the neighbours insist that on some nights the lift arrives at a floor that does not appear on the panel» immediately offers something to observe, check, connect or pursue.

It is also advisable that rumours should not be mere variations of true or false about one and the same statement. Different rumours may focus on different aspects of the phenomenon and produce a contradictory cultural image.

Suppose the PCs are investigating the laboratory on Hesiode Moor mentioned in a Piece of Conflict. The GD could have the following rumours to hand:

<u>Rumour:</u> _The laboratory was closed down thirty tetrads ago after all its researchers disappeared on the same night._
<u>Classification:</u> _Partially true, but biased._

<u>Rumour:</u> _Supplies still arrive at the laboratory once every cycle, although nobody admits to sending them._
<u>Classification:</u> _Probably true._

<u>Rumour:</u> _The laboratory is not abandoned: it simply stopped being in the same place._
<u>Classification:</u> _As hard to prove as to refute._

<u>Rumour:</u> _Those who have disappeared in recent years share a trait that the laboratory's researchers were trying to reproduce artificially._
<u>Classification:</u> _Unknown origin._

Observe that all four rumours could coexist. None obliges the GD to decide yet which one explains the Conflict. Instead, each provides a different way of relating to it.

### **Rumours and Pieces of Conflict**

A Rumour is not anything like a Piece of Conflict. It is worth keeping this distinction clean.

The rumour belongs to the cultural context: it expresses something that is said, remembered, suspected or interpreted. The Piece is a concrete manifestation that has acquired relevance within the Conflict. Nevertheless, investigating a rumour may lead to the discovery of a Piece, just as a Piece may acquire a new dimension when the PCs investigate its Rumourology.

If the characters hear that «the anemochronic clocks stop before a disappearance», they possess a rumour. If they later find in the laboratory an anemochronic clock stopped at exactly the hour at which one of the individuals they are investigating disappeared, that object may become a Piece of Conflict.

In this way, Rumourology does not replace the Pieces: it surrounds them with culture.

### **Pregenerated rumours and emergent rumours**

When building the Root, the GD may prepare a few rumours related to its NPCs, places, phenomena and potential Pieces. It is advisable to write them as flexible material, not as covert expositions of a truth that the players must reconstruct.

A pregenerated rumour may change during the adventure. If the players have established through their actions that a certain NPC has an unexpected relationship with the Conflict, a rumour prepared about another matter may shift towards that NPC. If an initially secondary Piece becomes the centre of their investigations, rumours may appear that were never written down.

The GD may also create entirely new rumours on the fly. A simple formula consists in asking oneself:

_What version would Numinia have built about this if it had spent a long time trying to explain it to itself without ever quite succeeding?_

The answer does not have to be correct. It has to be culturally plausible and narratively fertile.

### **The city as a living archive**

Rumourology allows Numinia to possess something that no GD's encyclopaedia could ever fully prepare: social memory.

Events do not exist only when the PCs reach them. They have been observed, misinterpreted, feared, trivialised, turned into jokes, debated by specialists, exploited politically and deformed by people who knew someone who claims to have spoken with someone who was there. The city has been telling itself about itself for a long time.

That is why rumours are not second-class information. They are cultural objects: testimonies of the relationship that Numinia maintains with whatever it does not yet understand.

And, within an emergent adventure, they possess an additional virtue. They allow the GD to introduce history without necessarily introducing an answer. They add a past without closing off the future; context without imposing interpretation; paths without pointing out which one must be walked.

Dialogic Resonance asks the players what the world may mean. The Pieces of Conflict provide them with things whose meaning they will have to construct. Rumourology adds a third voice, deliciously indiscreet: what the world itself has long been saying about them.

And a city that talks about itself, naturally, also lies from time to time. Otherwise, why would we have invented plazas?

## **Fragment 5: The Hermeneutic Action**

Numinia is full of things that seem to want to say something.

A face appears repeatedly in photographs taken decades apart. A creature always leaves three perfectly aligned stones behind after it disappears. An NPC speaks using only verbs in the future tense. In an abandoned plaza, a chair remains turned towards a wall on which there is nothing. A sculpture changes its posture every time someone utters a certain name.

These elements are not necessarily Pieces of Conflict or Rumours. Their fundamental characteristic is a different one: they carry an evident symbolic charge and a meaning still undetermined.

We shall call these elements Symbolic Figures.

A Symbolic Figure may be an object, an image, an event, a behaviour, a person, a spatial arrangement, a phrase, or any other manifestation singular enough to provoke the impression that its immediate appearance does not exhaust what it means.

The GD may introduce them in pregenerated form as part of the possibilities of the Root, or make them appear during the session out of the events and contributions of the players. As with other open elements of the adventure, their meaning must not be decided beforehand.

The Figure does not conceal a correct interpretation. It is a structure waiting to be interpreted.

### **Interpretation**

When the PCs come across a Symbolic Figure, the GD proposes a Hermeneutic Action. The players must interpret what they are contemplating from the perspective of their PCs.

But there is one fundamental condition: a PC does not interpret freely from any possible system. Their gaze is conditioned by their Position.

Here the Position works as an Interpretation Factor: it gives the player a concrete procedure through which to translate the Figure into meaning. The Game Master looks for an enigma; the Oneiromancer recognises an image of the unconscious; the Armonaut finds a trope; other PCs will use the systems of interpretation proper to their respective Positions.

This does not mean that two characters with the same Position have to reach the same conclusion. It means that they share an interpretative grammar, not a dictionary of answers.

Two Armonauts may see completely different metaphors in one and the same image. What they share is the decision to read it metaphorically.

### **Interpreting modifies what is interpreted**

The decisive particularity of the Hermeneutic Action is that its result is not limited to describing what the PC thinks.

The interpretation becomes part of the narrative nature of the Figure and, therefore, modifies or gives new dimension to its role in the story.

If an Oneiromancer interprets a flooded room as the representation of a repressed memory, that reading is not confined to the character's psychology. The GD will begin to integrate that relationship into the adventure. Perhaps the water will later respond to memories; perhaps another Piece of Conflict will reproduce the same image; perhaps we shall discover that someone connected with the Conflict is trying to forget something. The interpretation draws reality towards itself.

This does not mean that any passing notion immediately becomes an absolute truth. Just as happens with the anchors of confrontations, the interpretation must rest on what has been shown and must not contradict the fiction already established. But, within those limits, the GD takes on the commitment to make the proposed reading produce consequences.

Hermeneutics does not find out what the Figure meant. It decides what it begins to mean.

### **Multiple interpretations**

One and the same Figure may be interpreted by several PCs.

In fact, this is where one of the mechanic's most fertile possibilities appears.

Let us imagine a room in which there is a table laid for thirteen diners, but twelve of the chairs are covered in ash and one remains completely clean.

The Game Master could interpret the arrangement as a riddle: _there are thirteen positions and a single exception; the key lies in working out which rule allows that chair to be occupied_.

The Oneiromancer could interpret the twelve covered chairs as extinguished identities and the thirteenth as the persistence of a self that refuses to disappear.

The Armonaut could read the scene as a synecdoche: the table represents a whole community, and the empty chair, that which has survived its disappearance.

No reading has to invalidate the others.

The system of confrontations already establishes that two coherent interpretations may represent different angles of the same structure. The Hermeneutic Action carries that principle into the narrative heart of the adventure: the GD looks for a meaning capable of establishing relationships between the different readings.

Perhaps, then, the twelve chairs correspond to twelve missing individuals, and the clean one to a person who was supposed to disappear but managed to avoid it. For the Game Master there is a rule and an exception; for the Oneiromancer, an identity that resists being erased; for the Armonaut, an individual who represents the survival of the whole.

All three interpretations have taken part in the creation of what the image ends up being.

### **Hermeneutic synthesis**

When several characters perform a Hermeneutic Action on the same Figure, the GD should not simply choose the «best» interpretation. Their function is to seek a hermeneutic synthesis: a relationship that allows the greatest possible number of readings to manifest themselves simultaneously.

This does not require joining them together artificially. Some may acquire greater importance than others; one may determine the fundamental nature of the Figure while another becomes a secondary consequence or a cross-cutting story. What matters is that the pertinent interpretations leave their mark.

Here the same principle that governs collaborative creation comes into play once again: a proposal received seriously deserves a serious answer.

Interpretation is not an exam whose solution the GD knows. It is a proposal addressed to the world. And Numinia answers.

### **The Position as a lens**

The Hermeneutic Action turns each Position into an epistemological lens. The player does not simply receive the instruction to «interpret in keeping with their character», a formula as generous as it is useless. Each Position has a concrete procedure that allows work to begin.

Taking the Positions already established and their profiles ‒the Game Master oriented towards the enigma, the Armonaut towards rhetoric, the Ethnarch towards cultural patterns, the Archivist towards historical memory, the Hermeneut towards semiotics, etc.‒, they can be organised as follows.

Example: _the PCs face the image of an immense sea at night, but it is a sea of black stone, where the waves are rugged reliefs of the terrain._

Gatekeeper: «The sea is the passage from one period to another, a space of transit».

Pythia: «A sea of black stone will devour us when we face the path that calls us».

Ambassador: «The sea of black stone signals that a hard and austere crossing awaits us».

Ethnarch: «A sea of stone is the representation of a powerful and silent god».

Anacharchid: «In the sea of stone, what is motionless travels and what moves remains».

Oneiromancer: «The sea of stone represents a mother of rigid and censorious character».

### **Instruments of interpretation**

Whenever possible, the Hermeneutic Action can rely on a real instrument of interpretation. This prevents the lens of each Position from being reduced to an abstract indication and gives the player something concrete to play with.

A Game Master might have a deck of Tarot cards to hand and draw a spread for each interpretation, or own an anagram generator, or turn elements of the Figure into the pieces of a small puzzle; the Oneiromancer might turn to repertoires of symbols or a dictionary of dream interpretation; the Armonaut might carry a repertoire of rhetorical figures or a collection of metaphors, and search for the one they deem capable of explaining what they are contemplating.

The tool does not contain «the answer». It produces fertile constraints, in the same way that rules provide a field on which to play.

If a card drawn by the Game Master in a Tarot spread is _The Tower_, this does not mean that the Figure «is The Tower». It means that the player must discover what relationship they can build between the Figure and whatever that card allows them to think.

Thus, Hermeneutics upholds the fundamental principle we have been using throughout the chapter: the system does not provide solutions; it provides structures that make it possible to create solutions with resistance.

### **An important difference: icon, index and legisign**

The Hermeneutic Action starts from a fundamental idea: one and the same Symbolic Figure can produce meaning through very different relationships. As an operational reference, Numinia adopts three forms of relationship, drawing on what semiotics, following the theories of Peirce, regards as the three traditional types of sign: icon, index and legisign. This classification is used here as a tool of interpretation, and not as a strict reproduction of the categories of classical semiotics <sup>18</sup> .

In the icon, the sign refers to what it signifies through some form of resemblance. A silhouette can represent a mountain because it reproduces its shape; an image of darkness can evoke death because it shares with it certain culturally recognisable qualities. This logic is especially important for the interpretation of the Armonaut, whose rhetorical lens establishes figurative relationships between the Figure and another reality. Metaphor finds meaning by transferring properties from one term to another on a basis of resemblance or analogy.

18 Strictly speaking, Peirce calls the last type of sign a _symbol_, and points out its character as a legisign; in Numinia we shall use _legisign_ directly, reserving the word _symbol_ for its broader sense within the Hermeneutic Action, since throughout the theoretical history of interpretation the term _symbol_ has acquired a greater dimension of meaning.

In the index, on the other hand, meaning arises from a relationship of contiguity, connection or consequence. Smoke indicates fire not because it resembles it, but because a physical relationship exists between the two; a footprint signals someone's passing because it is the effect of their presence. This relationship is especially fertile for those Hermeneutic Lenses that read a Figure as the trace, consequence, provenance or manifestation of something else, such as the Ambassador.

The legisign, as it will be used operationally in this section, makes it possible to address relationships whose meaning depends on a code, a convention or a learned system rather than on an immediate resemblance or material connection. Its interest for the Hermeneutic Action appears above all in the interpretation of the Oneiromancer: the images of the unconscious need not resemble what they express. A house can become a body, water a birth, or a closed door a prohibition, through deep associations whose logic comes from psychic, cultural and personal experience. The relationship may seem arbitrary from the outside, but it is not without motivation for whoever produces it.

This makes it possible to draw an essential distinction between metaphor and unconscious image. When the Armonaut interprets the _Sea of Black Stone_ as an image of death and mourning, they build an analogical relationship: darkness, stillness and desolate expanse allow resemblances to be recognised between the two realities. When the Oneiromancer interprets that same sea as a rigid, censorious mother, the operation is different: they do not claim that a mother resembles a sea of stone, but establish a chain of associations ‒sea, origin, mother; stone, rigidity; black, prohibition or repression‒ whose meaning emerges from an unconscious logic.

### **When to introduce a Hermeneutic Figure**

The Hermeneutic Action possesses considerable narrative power and, precisely for that reason, should not be used constantly.

A Dialogic Resonance can fill in details of context relatively often. Rumourology can expand numerous culturalised elements. A Hermeneutic Figure, on the other hand, can profoundly alter the meaning of an adventure, because the players intervene directly in the nature of a symbolically charged element.

The GD should reserve it for moments when the fiction can bear a major fork.

It is especially appropriate when a central image of the Conflict appears, when a Piece holds exceptional symbolic content, after a Turbulence Phase, in the face of an especially significant anomaly, or when the adventure needs to open up a new field of interpretations. The GD can have these Symbolic Figures prepared in the Root, which gives them the chance to reflect carefully on their role in the adventure or to discard one, should the need arise.

Before introducing it, the GD should ask themselves a very simple question:

«Am I willing to let the players' interpretation change what this means and, with it, potentially change the adventure?»

If the answer is no, the conclusion is clear: it should not be presented as a Symbolic Figure.

### **The duty of consequence**

Once a Hermeneutic Action has been activated, the GD takes on the same commitment as with any other Space of Indeterminacy: they must allow interpretations to have consequences.

Not necessarily immediate ones. Nor necessarily literal ones.

An interpretation may reappear transformed into a Piece of Conflict; alter the attitude of an NPC; retrospectively explain an event; generate a cross-cutting story; suggest a new location; alter a future Symbolic Figure; or end up taking part in the overall interpretation of the Conflict during the Resolution.

The former adventure scheme already establishes that the players' theories may use Pieces, events and earlier contributions, and that the world responds to that interpretation instead of revealing a «hidden truth». Hermeneutics allows that construction to begin long before the Resolution.

Each Hermeneutic Action lays down layers of meaning.

And when the players finally look upon the complete adventure, part of what will seem to have been there from the very beginning will in fact have been born from the way they chose to look at it.

## **Fragment 6: Managing the Phases**

The Phases should not be understood as chapters that the GD writes before the adventure begins. They are narrative regimes: moments in which the purpose of the game, the rhythm of the narration and, above all, the distribution of initiative between the GD and the players change. The question the GD should ask when preparing a Phase is therefore not _«what is going to happen here?»_, but «what must be able to happen here, and what do I need to lay out so that the players can bring it about?»

This difference is fundamental. Preparing an Investigation Phase does not consist of writing a succession of scenes —first they will visit the Archive, then they will question the witness and finally they will discover the laboratory—, because nothing guarantees that the players will want to do any of those things. It consists of laying out possibilities: places, NPCs, Pieces of Conflict, Symbolic Figures, potential events, tensions, Dialogic Resonance questions and any other element that can be incorporated when the fiction finds the right moment. Some will appear; others will be transformed on coming into contact with the players' proposals; many will never be used at all.

For this reason, managing the Phases requires attending simultaneously to three variables: authority, rhythm and content. Authority determines who holds the greater initiative at each moment to produce the fiction. Rhythm determines how long a situation should be prolonged before its capacity to produce meaning begins to run dry. Content determines which elements it is advisable to introduce, recover or leave in suspense. All three variables change from one Phase to another.

This produces a kind of breathing of the adventure. The GD initially takes the voice, cedes it little by little, lets the players expand the fiction, gathers it back to put in order what has been produced, hands it over again during the action and finally regains considerable control when the conflict enters Resolution. There is, therefore, no constant distribution of authority. Directing Numinia also consists of knowing when to speak, when to ask, when to intervene and when to have the exquisite courtesy to keep quiet.

The transitions between Phases are part of that breathing. The GD can always simply announce _«the Investigation Phase begins»_, but, whenever possible, it is preferable to make the change visible through the fiction itself. An event, an arrival, a decision, a journey or even a change of scene can naturally alter the narrative regime of the game. The seams exist; there is no need to show them off every time.

### **Presentation Phase: delivering the conflict**

In the Presentation, the GD clearly holds the main voice. It is the moment closest to traditional narration and may take on a certain literary quality: an opening sequence built carefully enough to present the immediate world of the adventure, establish its tone and bring the Starting Conflict into view. The GD should have no qualms about describing, arranging events, playing NPCs and steering the players' attention for a while. This is the entrance gate, and someone must have the kindness to open it. The GD approaches this Phase as though the players were reading ‒or listening to‒ the first chapter of a novel.

But holding the main voice does not mean monopolising it. The Presentation must already begin to show what kind of game Numinia is. One or two instances of Dialogic Resonance make it possible to open small Spaces of Indeterminacy within a predominantly directed sequence: _What detail of the Ministerial Hall makes you think someone has left the place in a hurry?_, _Why do you immediately recognise the individual waiting by the door?_ The GD establishes the situation; the players begin to contaminate it delightfully with their own contributions.

It is wise to be especially careful with the amount of information. The Presentation should offer enough material to speculate on, but not so much as to conclude. The established facts should be clear; their relationships, not necessarily. If three Numinids have disappeared, an agent of the Summa Archive has come to the scene and the same Veil anomaly has been recorded in all three disappearances, those facts can be perfectly well established. What they mean together must remain open.

Its duration should be relatively short. The danger of an overlong Presentation is that it turns into a lecture by the GD on the marvellous adventure they have prepared while everyone else politely contemplates their enthusiasm. When the players know the conflict, possess several significant pieces of information and have enough material to formulate initial interpretations, the Presentation has fulfilled its function.

The transition can be brought about by a small event that closes the exposition: the NPC who summoned them leaves, an unexpected message arrives, they are handed an object related to the conflict, or they are simply left alone after receiving the commission. The world stops speaking to them. Now it is their turn to speak about it.

### **Deliberation Phase: listening to the first hypotheses**

The Deliberation reverses the previous relationship. The GD cedes the initiative to the players. The PCs provide the situation from which the conversation takes place, but it is the players who must partly step out of them in order to observe the adventure from the outside and formulate their Starting Hypotheses.

This distinction must be explicit. The GD can place the PCs arguing around a table in the Hyperborean Tavern, but when they ask _what do you think is going on?_ they must make it clear whether they are addressing the character or the player. The difference may seem small until it stops being so. It is one thing for a Legionary to suspect the Summa Archive because experience has taught them to distrust certain institutions; it is another for their player to propose that the Archive is concealing a clandestine investigation because they consider that narrative possibility fertile.

The Starting Hypotheses do not require consensus. On the contrary: divergence is valuable. One player may suspect that the disappearances are the result of an experiment; another, that the disappeared share some piece of knowledge; a third, that someone is trying to move them out of Numinia. The GD must listen to the hypotheses like someone receiving seeds, not like someone marking an exam. None is automatically confirmed, but all of them offer materials that may later become details, cross-cutting stories, Pieces of Conflict or even central elements of the adventure.

The GD should stimulate the debate without steering its conclusions. They may recall a forgotten detail, point out a contradiction or ask a player what leads them to uphold a given hypothesis, but they must not surreptitiously lead the conversation towards what they had imagined in the Root.

The Deliberation should be brief, though not hurried. Each player needs time to formulate a consistent hypothesis and test it against the others. When arguments begin to repeat themselves, the positions are clear and the conversation stops producing new possibilities, the Phase has come to its end.

Then a single question is enough to return the players to their PCs and open the next Phase:

_Very well. Where do you begin to investigate?_

### **Investigation Phase: administering the expansion**

The Investigation is normally the longest Phase of the adventure, and the one in which emergent creation reaches its greatest breadth. It should not be understood exclusively in a detective sense. To investigate here means to increase the group's capacity to understand and face the Conflict: exploring places, questioning NPCs, seeking information, obtaining resources, testing hypotheses, exposing themselves to events, interpreting Symbolic Figures or discovering possibilities for action that did not exist before.

The GD's position changes radically. They no longer lead the players through a sequence, but administer the consequences of their decisions. The players choose where to go, what to investigate, whom to speak to and which elements they consider relevant. The GD provides resistance, response and continuity.

This does not mean adopting a notarial passivity. An Investigation needs events. Things must happen. An NPC may lie, a facility may suffer an anomaly, someone may follow the PCs, a creature may appear, a faction may intervene, an interpretation may produce unexpected consequences. There may be confrontations, Action Rolls, Heterocosmic Rolls and scenes of great intensity. Freedom of initiative does not in any way mean an absence of dramatic pressure.

Here several of the tools described earlier find their natural territory. Dialogic Resonance makes it possible to build spaces and situations; Rumourology expands elements that have acquired a cultural dimension; the Hermeneutic Action turns certain Symbolic Figures into engines of meaning; and the Heterocosmic allows the players to introduce realities capable of considerably altering the investigation. The GD's task consists of relating these contributions, recovering some of them later on and allowing others to disappear when they are no longer fertile.

But the main instrument for regulating the length of this Phase is the Pieces of Conflict. The GD must learn to recognise three different moments: when an emergent contribution deserves to become a Piece; when it is advisable to introduce one of the Pieces prepared in the Root; and when the moment has come to present the Direction Piece.

They should not hand them out according to a calendar. A Piece appears when a scene has produced something that deserves to acquire structural weight. If a player spontaneously steals an agent's wallet and the GD places inside it the card of an abandoned café connected to a disappearance, the player's own action has created the occasion for a Piece to be born. At another moment, an investigation that is beginning to lose direction may be the perfect occasion to bring back a pre-generated Piece.

The Direction Piece requires even greater judgement. Introduced too early, it impoverishes the Investigation by providing a path when possibilities have barely begun to open up; too late, it turns freedom into drift. It should appear when the adventure has already produced enough meaning for a direction to feel stimulating rather than restrictive.

Its appearance normally signals that the Investigation can end, but it does not compel it to. The players may want to keep looking for Pieces, test another hypothesis or settle some pending question. The GD should allow them to do so as long as the investigation keeps producing relevant content. When the scenes begin to repeat information, decisions no longer open new possibilities or the energy at the table starts to flag, the GD is entitled to bring about the transition.

The Investigation should be broad; not interminable. Even the most exquisite mystery ends up bearing a certain resemblance to an administrative meeting when it is investigated for too long.

### **Reflection Phase: turning possibilities into a direction**

Reflection constitutes a second contraction of the rhythm. After the expansion of the Investigation, the players need to pause, observe what they have gathered and decide what it means to them. Its structure is reminiscent of the Deliberation, but there is one fundamental difference: now they must reach a shared interpretation.

The GD should create that space of rest within the fiction. The PCs may return to their lodgings, take shelter after a pursuit, occupy a safe room in the installation they are exploring or gather somewhere they can examine everything they have discovered. The stillness of the scene should deliberately contrast with the preceding activity.

Once that frame is established, the players take control again. They set out the Pieces they have obtained, recover their Starting Hypotheses ‒which may have changed radically‒, incorporate everything that happened during the Investigation and build a common theory about the Conflict. Unlike in the Deliberation, this time it is not enough to keep several possibilities alive: they must decide which one to adopt.

Consensus does not mean discovering «the correct answer». It means choosing which interpretation they will turn into a principle of action.

Let us imagine that the Pieces make it possible to argue that the missing are being taken to a laboratory on the Hesiode Moor, but there are several possible explanations: the Summa Archive is carrying out experiments on them; it is trying to protect them from some anomaly; or an infiltrated faction is using abandoned installations of the Archive. The players do not need to prove which one is true. They need to decide what they believe and what they will do because they believe it.

From this a concrete objective must emerge: to enter the laboratory and free the missing; to infiltrate it in order to identify those responsible; to destroy the installation; to negotiate with those who occupy it; or any other direction coherent with the theory they have built.

The GD should intervene little. They may put information in order, recall forgotten Pieces or ask the players to pin down a contradiction, but not resolve it for them. When there is an agreed theory and an objective that can be acted upon, the Reflection is over.

And then the adventure expands once more, but no longer in every direction.

### **Action Phase: accelerating without closing**

In the Action, the players retain a good part of the initiative they held during the Investigation, but the pressure of time changes radically. They are no longer slowly broadening their understanding of the Conflict: they are acting upon it. The narration must gain speed, risk and a growing sense of irreversibility.

The GD once again occupies a paradoxical position: they cede creative authority while increasing their control over the rhythm. The players decide how to act; the GD makes sure that whatever they act upon responds with energy.

Scenes should begin closer to their point of tension and end sooner. Irrelevant journeys can be compressed. Consequences must produce new situations quickly. If the PCs secretly enter a laboratory, there is no need to play out every corridor unless one of them holds a meaningful decision. What matters is the door they should not be able to open, the researcher who recognises them, the chamber where they find something incompatible with their theory, the alarm that has just gone off.

Collaborative creation does not disappear just because the fiction accelerates. On the contrary: the players' contributions must go on transforming the adventure. An intervention of the Heterocosmic, an unexpected interpretation or a particularly fertile decision may modify the setting of the Action as much as they previously modified the Investigation. The GD must not protect the climax they had imagined from the very players who are, precisely, creating it.

Dialogic Resonance takes on an especially intense form here through the Crossroads, a dynamic explained in the following fragment. Questions no longer merely broaden the context: they may force the swift determination of elements that alter a situation in motion.

Before beginning this Phase, the GD should be clear about which conditions indicate that the action has reached its end. It may be a single objective or several: reaching the heart of the laboratory, freeing certain captives, activating a mechanism, getting to an NPC, surviving a collapse. These conditions may be known to the players when the nature of the situation advises it, or remain implicit when that proves more effective narratively.

The Action is designed for a medium duration: more developed than the Presentation, Deliberation or Reflection, but more concentrated than the Investigation. It is not measured by the number of scenes but by the density of events. If the Investigation widens, the Action compresses.

And when the group has reached the point where it is no longer trying to reach the heart of the Conflict but confronting its definitive consequences, the Resolution should appear almost without needing to be announced.

### **Resolution Phase: handing the adventure over to the Prism**

In the Resolution, the GD recovers a clearly leading position, but not in order to impose an outcome. Their function consists in presenting the decisive situations and administering the consequences of the Prism Rolls. The feeling sought is a peculiar one: after having built, interpreted and actively steered a good part of the adventure, the players reach a point at which what they have set in motion begins to respond of its own accord.

The Phase should be shorter than the Action and heighten the sense of vertigo even further. It is not advisable to introduce extensive new lines of investigation or secondary conflicts here. Everything must tend towards consequence.

The four Resolution situations must correspond to the characters of the Heterocosmic operators: alethic, deontic, epistemic and axiological. The GD should not present them as four mechanical boxes to be ceremoniously ticked off. They must search within the outcome for the fictional moment in which each question becomes pertinent: what can or cannot come to happen; which rule, obligation or prohibition comes into play; what can come to be known; and what value is acquired by what is taking place.

The main difficulty in directing lies precisely in the moment of introducing each Prism Roll. If it appears before the fiction has truly posed its question, it will feel artificial; if it appears after the scene has already resolved it narratively, it will be redundant. The roll must happen when the question exists and its answer can still transform the outcome.

Here it is essential to remember that Prism Rolls do not decide whether the players «win» or «lose» the adventure. They determine by which path its consequences materialise. An unfavourable result may produce a painful, costly or deeply uncomfortable reality; a favourable one may bring the story closer to the group's intentions. Neither invalidates what has been played. The adventure is not an exam whose mark is revealed at the end.

The GD must, moreover, allow the results to genuinely modify the situation. It is not advisable to decide beforehand what the outcome will be and use the Rolls to colour it in. Each result must close some possibilities and open others, so that the second Roll takes place already within the reality transformed by the first, the third within the consequences of the previous ones, and so on. The Resolution is cumulative.

Hence its particular feeling of loss of control. The players have built the theory, chosen the path and carried out their action; now they watch how the consequences link together and how Numinia responds to everything they have done.

The GD once again takes the lead voice, but there is a fundamental difference with respect to the Presentation. At the beginning they narrated possibilities still intact; now they narrate the consequences of a story that belongs to everyone.

## **Fragment 7: Other narrative resources**

In addition to the fundamental structures of collaborative creation, the GD may turn to other narrative resources intended to intervene at specific moments of the adventure: when a consequence needs to acquire meaning, when several proposals call out for an unexpected relationship, or when the fiction offers an especially fertile opportunity to return part of its authorship to the players. These resources are not obligatory steps, nor should they be incorporated systematically; they are tools of opportunity, procedures the GD can introduce when circumstances make them fruitful for opening new possibilities without interrupting the course of the narration. As always in Numinia, they are best used to cultivate the ground, not to decide in advance what must grow in it.

### **The Crossroads**

The Crossroads is a special form of Dialogic Resonance that is especially well suited to the Action Phase. Unlike an ordinary Resonance, it does not open a question for the players to complete freely; instead it presents three possible interpretations of an element that has already acquired relevance within the adventure.

The three options must be plausible given what has happened up to that moment and, above all, they must be equally compromising. A Crossroads does not pose one correct answer, one wrong one and a third especially prudent one. Nor does it offer a favourable option, a neutral one and a harmful one. It presents three different ways of complicating the fiction, each capable of leading the story somewhere different.

The players do not choose which consequence they would rather suffer. They choose what kind of truth has just entered the adventure.

Let us suppose that the PCs have entered a collective dream to find an Oneiromancer who went astray there many years ago. The dream is being interfered with by an ancient security protocol associated with the Veil and created to protect the memory of Numinia. Within the dream space, that protocol manifests as Elizabeth, a girl of about ten who seems entirely unaware of her true nature.

During the Action Phase, one of the PCs tries to stop the structure of the dream from breaking down by means of a Competence Roll and obtains an unfavourable result. The GD decides that the consequence will not simply be that «the dream becomes more unstable». Elizabeth has already acquired enough importance to become the centre of a Crossroads:

What is really happening with Elizabeth?

1. Elizabeth is completely mad.

2. Elizabeth is lying.

3. Elizabeth is irreversibly broken.

Any of the three answers introduces a significant difficulty, but none of them produces the same adventure.

If she is mad, the protocol goes on trying to fulfil its function through a distorted perception of what it must protect. If she is lying, one must ask what Elizabeth really knows and why she hides her nature. If she is broken, the problem is no longer only understanding her: there is a security structure of Numinia's memory whose deterioration perhaps cannot be reversed.

The GD does not need to have decided beforehand which of these possibilities was true. Whichever one the players choose becomes true, and from that moment on the GD must integrate it with the same seriousness as any other contribution consolidated during the adventure.

**The Crossroads as consequence**

The Crossroads works especially well when it appears linked to a series of Rolls during the Action Phase.

The Action usually contains situations in which the PCs must maintain a condition while they try to achieve their main objective: containing an anomaly, preventing a structure from collapsing, keeping a connection alive, stabilising a mechanism, protecting someone, keeping an access open or preventing an external force from finally prevailing.

The GD can articulate this pressure through successive Characteristic Rolls or, preferably when the fiction allows it, through Competence Rolls supported by some object, system or resource present in the scene. Competences are particularly suitable because they oblige the player to explain how they use their specialised knowledge to face the challenge.

For example, while several PCs travel through the collective dream in search of the Oneiromancer, another may use a device to keep their connection with the outside world stable. While the group crosses an installation that is collapsing, someone must keep its cooling system running. While they try to close a fracture in the Veil, another PC preserves the geometry of the space through Virtual Architecture.

These rolls are not necessarily confrontations, nor do they form a task independent of the Phase's objective. They are lines of containment: actions that prevent the environment from deteriorating while the characters try to complete what they have really come to do.

When one of these Rolls yields a Failure result, the GD sets a Crossroads in motion instead of unilaterally imposing its meaning. The Crossroads need not be directly related to the action that produced the failed roll. On the other hand, a Critical Failure with consequences does not rule out the appearance of the Crossroads.

The Critical Failure determines that something has become complicated.

The Crossroads allows the players to determine what that complication means.

Thus chance keeps its function ‒something has gone wrong‒, but authorship of the consequence is once again shared around the table.

**How to build a Crossroads**

To prepare a Crossroads, the GD must always start from an element already charged with meaning. It may be an NPC, a Piece of Conflict, a place, an organisation, an object, a Symbolic Figure or any other aspect the players have learnt to recognise as relevant. The Crossroads is not the right moment to introduce three possibilities about an element nobody remembers: it must act on something whose transformation matters.

Next, the GD formulates three complete statements about that element. Not three actions the characters could carry out, but three different ways of understanding what has just happened.

The options must meet three conditions: they must be compatible with the known fiction; they must produce clearly different narrative consequences; and none may be manifestly more comfortable or desirable than the others. A good test consists in asking oneself: «If the players choose any of the three, am I sincerely interested in discovering what adventure arises from it?» If an option is there only so that nobody chooses it, the Crossroads is badly built.

For example, if an ancient device of the Summa Archive that is guiding the PCs begins to behave inexplicably, the GD could pose:

The device is leading you to the wrong place because…

1. Someone is modifying its signal from outside

2. It is following an old version of the map

3. It considers that your true destination is not the one you believe.

All three options are problematic. All three generate different questions. None of them amounts simply to «the apparatus is malfunctioning».

That is a Crossroads.

**Choosing and moving on**

The choice should be made relatively quickly. The Crossroads belongs especially to a Phase whose energy depends on movement; if each appearance produces twenty minutes of metaphysical deliberation, it will end up moving at roughly the speed of a land-registry procedure.

The GD may address the decision to the player whose action triggered the Crossroads, or to the whole group when the consequence clearly affects everyone. What matters is that it is made explicit who has the authority to choose before the options are presented.

Once the choice has been made, the GD should not immediately explain all its consequences. It is enough to make visible the first manifestation of the new reality and continue the Action.

The players have chosen that Elizabeth is lying.

The GD smiles, describes how the girl stops crying for an instant, looks straight at the PC and says:

—You shouldn't remember my name.

And the scene continues.

The Crossroads does not serve to halt the Action and explain it.

It serves to make the Action change direction while it is still running.

### **The Counterpoint**

During an adventure, two contributions from the players will often appear that seem hard to reconcile: two Starting Hypotheses, two interpretations of a situation, two consequences created through the Heterocosmic, or even two details introduced at very different moments.

The usual procedure might consist in choosing one and abandoning the other. The Counterpoint occasionally invites the opposite: to assume that both are true and ask the players to discover the relationship that allows them to coexist.

This is not a Hermeneutic Synthesis, since it does not necessarily operate on a Symbolic Figure or on the lenses of the Positions. It is a broader narrative tool intended to recover scattered contributions and make them enter into dialogue.

Let us imagine that during the adventure one player established that the agent of the Summa Archive seems to be protecting the missing, while another maintained that this same agent is hiding information about the laboratory on the Hesiode Moor. Later on, the GD may bring back both proposals:

«We now know that both things are true: the agent is protecting the missing and is deliberately hiding information about the laboratory. What is the relationship between the two?»

The question does not ask the players to choose one explanation over another. It requires them to build a third relationship capable of containing the previous two.

Perhaps the agent hides the laboratory precisely because the missing are protected there. Perhaps they are protecting some of the missing from those who control the installation. Perhaps they are trying to prevent the Archive from discovering that certain individuals were never abducted.

Once again, there is no foreseen answer.

The Counterpoint has an especially interesting virtue: it rewards narrative memory. It makes old contributions, even apparently incompatible ones, return and acquire a new function. In a game which establishes that actions and creations constantly reorganise the world, this recovery of scattered elements fits very naturally with the general logic of Numinia.

I would not use it as often as Dialogic Resonance. The Counterpoint works best when two elements already carry enough weight for joining them to be surprising. Using it constantly would produce a fiction where absolutely everything is related to absolutely everything, and even the Veil has the right to rest on the odd Sunday.

## **Fragment 8: Imbalance of the Gearwork, an anomaly**

The Imbalance of the Gearwork is an exception within the ordinary architecture of the Phases. It belongs to none of them and, at the same time, it can burst into any of them when the accumulation of Heterocosmic Rolls has advanced the Resonance Marker far enough and, through it, the Plot Clock. When the clock reaches its limit, the adventure is momentarily interrupted and Numinia responds to everything the players have introduced into the world through a reorganisation of the Veil.

For the GD, this means something very concrete: the Imbalance must not be treated as a complication added to the Phase in progress, but as a self-contained mini-adventure embedded within it.

The main narration is suspended. Space dissolves, the PCs are transported to another time, to another state of the same place or to an alternative echo of reality, and for one complete scene they must pass through a situation with its own beginning, development and closure. When the fold ends, they return to the main adventure and the Phase continues from the point at which it had been interrupted, though no longer under exactly the same conditions.

The Imbalance does not destroy the continuity of the adventure: it folds it in order to return it transformed. Its purpose must be to reveal, recontextualise or significantly distort some of the elements that were already present in the Conflict. The Game System establishes precisely that the leap may lead to the past, to the future or to an alternative version of the same space, and that it must provide a relevant new perspective before the Refitting.

### **An unexpected adventure, not an improvised one**

The Imbalance is unpredictable as to when it will appear, but that does not oblige the GD to face it completely empty-handed.

During the preparation of the Root, the GD should develop a few potential proposals for an Imbalance: images of the Dark Age related to the Conflict, future versions of certain places, alternative scenes of important NPCs, events that could be shown from another point of view or strange conditions capable of reconfiguring a location. As with any other potential proposal, these materials should not be written as closed scenes nor committed to in advance. They are adaptable pieces the GD may use if, when the Imbalance arrives, they still make sense.

The GD might prepare, for example, the possibility of showing the laboratory on Hesiode Moor during an earlier era; but if, when the Imbalance is triggered, the laboratory has barely acquired any relevance and the players have made the old café in the Corridor of Memory the core of the adventure, it would be preferable to abandon that idea and build the fold around the café.

The principle is the same one that governs all preparation in Numinia: prepare possibilities, not obligations.

The Imbalance must respond to the adventure that is happening, not to the adventure the GD imagined before starting.

### **Choosing what it should recontextualise**

When the Plot Clock reaches its limit, the GD should quickly observe which elements have acquired the greatest density during the session and choose one of them as the centre of gravity of the Imbalance.

The best candidates are usually elements that have already generated questions: a Piece of Conflict whose relationship is not yet understood, an NPC whose motivations remain open, a Symbolic Figure interpreted in different ways, a place that has appeared repeatedly, a Heterocosmic creation that has acquired unexpected relevance or a hypothesis of the players that still lacks sufficient support.

The Imbalance should not introduce a completely unrelated story. Its strength comes from making something known appear in an impossible light.

If the players have found the card from the abandoned café, for example, the fold could carry them to that same establishment several tetrads earlier, when it was still open. There they do not need to discover «the truth» about the disappearances. It would be enough for them to observe that the same group of customers always occupies the same tables, that a young agent of the Summa Archive comes in every night at a set hour or that on the wall hangs a painting whose motif the PCs have already found elsewhere.

The Imbalance must not resolve the Conflict.

It must make it impossible to interpret it exactly as before.

### **Building the mini-adventure**

Although it lasts a single scene, the Imbalance needs a recognisable structure. The GD can organise it through three very simple movements:

Entry: the PCs discover where —or when— they have been transported and what extraordinary condition governs the place.

Tension: a situation arises that demands intervening, deciding, interpreting or confronting something. It is not enough to wander passively through a vision. The players must do something within the fold.

Closure: an action, discovery or event brings the scene to an end and provokes the Refitting.

This structure allows the Imbalance to have an entity of its own without becoming a second complete adventure. It should feel intense, strange and concentrated.

For example, the PCs may appear in a future version of the Prytaneum, completely abandoned. They discover that all its entrances are sealed except one and that, beyond it, someone is pronouncing the names of the disappeared. To reach the hall they must decide what to do about an anomaly blocking the way; inside they find a known NPC, aged by many decades, who refuses to explain how they got there but hands them a significant phrase or object. As soon as they receive it, the space comes undone and they return to the present.

They do not need to know whether they have beheld a real future, a possibility or a resonance. What matters is that the adventure now has a new layer of meaning.

Do not turn the Imbalance into a definitive revelation

There is a considerable temptation: to use the Imbalance as permission to explain what had until then been kept open. Precisely because it allows the past, the future or alternative realities to be shown, the GD might feel that the moment has come to reveal «what really happened».

It is best to resist.

The Imbalance must provide relevant information, not a canonical solution. It can show an event from the past, but its relationship with the present may remain uncertain. It can reveal a future consequence, but without establishing that it is inevitable. It can show an alternative version of the Conflict, but without declaring that this version is truer than the current one.

This is especially important in an adventure based on emergent mystery. If the GD uses the fold to reveal the true explanation, they retrospectively destroy the space of interpretation the players were building.

It is far more fertile to show the players something indisputable whose meaning remains open to dispute.

### **Preserving shared authorship within the fold**

Because of its spectacular nature, the Imbalance may seem an occasion for the GD to take back the narration entirely. The GD should not do so.

Although the GD has a stronger presence in establishing the new time, space and condition of the scene, the mini-adventure still belongs to the same system of collaborative creation. It may contain Dialogic Resonances, players' decisions, hermeneutic interpretations and actions capable of transforming what the GD had prepared.

In fact, the unstable nature of the fold makes it an especially fitting space for Resonance:

_You recognise this building, although you have never seen it like this. What detail proves that it belongs to an earlier era?_

_Among all the people in the room there is one you know should not be here. Who is it?_

_Something in this future proves that your current theory was incomplete. What is it?_

As always, the question contains an assertion and a Space of Indeterminacy. The Imbalance does not suspend the rules of shared creation; it subjects them to extraordinary pressure.

### **What must change on returning**

The Refitting should not simply return the PCs to the same narrative state they were in before the fold. If nothing has changed, the Imbalance will have been a beautiful decorative interlude and little more.

At least one significant residue must survive: a piece of information, an intuition, a behaviour, a perception, a new relationship between elements or a minor alteration of the context. The earlier material of the System already establishes precisely that the return must produce a new perspective capable of recontextualising the adventure.

That residue may even become a Piece of Conflict if it has sufficient concreteness, relevance and openness. It may also modify an existing Piece, transform a hypothesis, introduce a new cross-cutting story or alter the meaning of a Symbolic Figure already interpreted.

What matters is that the players can say:

_After that, we can no longer understand this in the same way._

That is the true consequence of the Imbalance.

### **The Imbalance within each Phase**

Although it can occur whenever the Plot Clock reaches its limit, not all Phases offer the same likelihood nor demand the same way of handling it.

Most commonly it will appear during the Investigation Phase. It is the longest Phase, the one in which the players use the Heterocosmic most freely and where the most emergent material accumulates. Here the Imbalance can act as a powerful recontextualisation: altering a hypothesis, producing a new Piece, recovering a forgotten element or opening an unexpected direction.

It is also especially natural during the Action Phase. In that case, the GD must make sure the mini-adventure retains the energy of the Phase. The fold should not become a long contemplative rest, but should intensify the vertigo: the PCs may appear before a future version of what they are trying to prevent, relive an antecedent of the present confrontation or pass through an impossible variant of the same space. On returning, the Action continues, but the decisions made within the Imbalance may have altered its meaning.

In the Resolution Phase it is much less likely. The Heterocosmic becomes riskier as the adventure accumulates Resonance, but the very nature of the Resolution considerably reduces the opportunities for new Heterocosmic Rolls: the GD is by now steering the consequences of a fiction that has been largely built. What was not created before now has very little room to appear.

If, exceptionally, the Imbalance occurs here, it must be extraordinarily concise and directly linked to the denouement. It is not advisable to use it to open a new plotline when the adventure is closing its possibilities. Its function should be to recontextualise the Resolution in progress, not to replace it.

In Presentation, Deliberation or Reflection, its appearance is practically impossible, both because of the brevity of those Phases and because of the very nature of these Phases, which do not lend themselves to Heterocosmic Rolls.

### **Managing the pace**

The Imbalance interrupts the Phase, but it must not break the overall pace of the adventure.

During Investigation it can afford to breathe a little more and form a scene with relatively ample development. During Action it must be sharper. During Resolution, if it appears, almost lightning-fast.

The GD should measure its duration by a very simple question:

_Has the mini-adventure already produced the recontextualisation it needed?_

When the answer is yes, the GD must close it.

It is not necessary to explain everything that appears in the fold or to exhaust its possibilities. In fact, leaving some element not understood reinforces its character. The Imbalance works best when it seems to contain more world than the scene has had time to show.

### **Returning to the Phase**

Once the Refitting has taken place, the GD immediately returns attention to the Phase that had been suspended.

It is advisable to briefly recall the last situation before the fold and point out what has changed:

_«You are back in the back room of the café. The computer is still on. Only a few seconds have passed. But now you recognise the symbol blinking beside the encrypted file: it is the same one you have just seen chiselled above the door of the laboratory, many tetrads in the future.»_

And the Investigation goes on.

A new Phase does not begin simply because an Imbalance has occurred. The fold works as an insertion within the main structure, a small adventure born of the accumulation of play that afterwards settles back upon it.

In that sense, the Imbalance is perhaps the most radical expression of one of the central principles of Numinia: the players' freedom not only modifies the story; it can force the world itself to reorganise in order to contain it.

And the GD must not fear that interruption.

The GD must receive it for what it is: the moment when the adventure, saturated with everything the table has created, needs to dream itself for a few minutes before it can go on.

## **Annex: Advice for the GD**

The Modular Template is not a rigid index but a narrative organism, a way of structuring adventures that dialogues with the fractal, symbolic and poetic nature of Numinia. Each Phase is a chamber that holds possibilities, and each adventure is a unique journey that arises from the encounter between what the GD prepares and what the players reveal.

In Numinia, truth is not pursued: it is built. A path is not followed: it is traced. A conflict is not resolved: it is interpreted.

The Root is the tool that allows all of this to happen with coherence and depth.

In Numinia, the GD is neither an all-powerful narrator nor a secret scriptwriter: the GD is a facilitator, a person who sustains the fiction so that the players can explore and transform it. Think of your role as that of a correspondent of the world: you present scenes, give voice to the inhabitants of the city, embody the dilemmas and propose challenges. The rest will be decided by the players through their actions and contributions.

### **Essential advice**

1. Prepare little, listen a lot

You do not need to write a novel beforehand. Arrive with an initial idea —a mystery, a conflict, a strange place— and let the players complete the picture. The story is built by everyone together.

2. Ask questions, return consequences

When in doubt, do not settle it yourself: ask the table. “What does this neighbourhood look like from inside?” “What rumour is going around among the inhabitants?” The answers create living material. Then return those ideas in the form of tangible consequences.

3. Think in scenes, not in closed plots

Design open situations: a disputed archive, a corrupted relic, a district at war. Do not decide the ending: let the choices of the players themselves determine the ultimate truth.

4. Bring everything to life

Give personality to every element: a square that whispers, an archive that defends itself, an NPC with an unshakeable passion. The more vivid the details, the easier it will be for the players to interact.

5. Embrace failure

In Numinia, failing does not stop the story, but opens new paths. If an action does not go well, turn the consequence into an unexpected twist that expands the adventure.

6. Support the players' voice

Every contribution enriches the cosmos of the story and of Numinia. Encourage the players to invent districts, factions, rituals. Do not correct: adapt, and make those ideas have real consequences.

7. Play to discover

Do not come with the story already written: your role is to discover it together with everyone else. Let yourself be surprised. If you already know the ending, you are preventing Numinia from breathing.

### **Immediate resources**

  - Use tension clocks: draw a circle divided into segments and mark them off when a danger advances. Visual and easy to follow.

  - Create hidden agendas: give NPCs simple goals («protect their neighbourhood», «gain power», «discover the truth») and let them act accordingly.

  - Reuse ideas from the table: if someone describes a symbol, make it reappear later as a key.

  - Always keep an open question for the players: it is the best seed of story.

### **GD principles**

In _Powered by the Apocalypse_ (PbtA) games, the GD does not merely prepare a story, but acts according to an agenda and a set of play principles. This means there is no need to memorise complex rules or invent perfect plots: it is enough to remember a few clear guidelines that indicate how to play the role and what decisions to make at each moment.

Numinia adopts that same philosophy. Your principles as GD are practical reminders: simple phrases that condense how to facilitate the session. They are not rigid rules, but compasses. Their purpose is to help you always keep the spirit of the game: the shared creation of a cosmos in constant transformation.

**The GD principles in Numinia**

  - <u>Ask questions, never give all the answers.</u>

Let the players complete the fiction. A city is richer if it is described by every voice together.

  - <u>Leave gaps: the city is completed by the players' voice.</u>

Do not try to define everything in advance: gaps are spaces for the tale to grow at the table.

  - <u>Bring everything to life: neighbourhoods, objects and factions have a will of their own.</u>

Numinia is not a passive setting: every element reacts and pushes the story.

  - <u>Play to discover, not to impose.</u>

The ending is not written. Be as surprised as the players: that is the essence of shared narration.

  - <u>Turn every failure into an opportunity for transformation.</u>

A mistake does not stop the story: it opens it towards new paths, dilemmas and twists.

  - <u>Make the world react: every action generates consequences.</u>

The cosmos always responds to what the characters do. Every decision leaves a mark.

  - <u>Think in scenes, not in closed plots.</u>

Create tense, open situations. Do not prepare outcomes: let the choices determine the course.

  - <u>Be a fan of the characters: show their virtues and put their weaknesses to the test.</u>

The interest lies not in defeating the players, but in seeing how their characters shine when they face the conflict.

  - <u>Bring the strange into the everyday: the impossible is part of Numinia.</u>

Remember that this alternative world is ruled by different laws: let the fantastic emerge in any corner.

  - <u>Value every contribution: each idea from the table is a narrative seed.</u>

When a player adds a detail, make it important. Reincorporate it, transform it, give it weight in the story.

This list is not a script to follow step by step, but a framework for action. When you do not know what to do, return to these principles: they will remind you how to keep the session alive, even in unforeseen moments.
