# **CHAPTER 4**

# **GAME SYSTEM**
**The invisible structure**

« _The streets of the Ouroboros District looked like an endless labyrinth, where the walls curved as if the city were folding in on itself. There, among narrow alleys and hidden squares, lay the Plaza of the Shifting Murals, a place where the surroundings themselves seemed to resist the laws of order._

_It was said that the murals covering its walls were echoes of the Akasha, fragments of the code that shaped the very reality of Numinia. They were not merely images: they were living symbols, which moved, dissolved and were reborn with every change in the city. There, beneath the stony gaze of an ancient monument, Iskar, the sharp-eyed veteran Exegete, stopped dead. His young apprentice, Naevin, looked at the tangle of signs in bewilderment._

_‒What do you see? ‒asked Iskar._

_Naevin leaned in, watching how the symbols changed colour and shape, as if each figure faded away to make room for another. In one corner of the mural, a series of winding lines merged into a web of dots, forming what looked like a path._

_‒A pattern ‒said Naevin‒. It looks like a path towards..._

_‒It looks like one ‒Iskar interrupted him‒. But look more closely._

_The young man drew nearer, straining his eyes. The lines he had taken for a trail were in fact a labyrinth, with false paths that led nowhere. At the other end of the mural, a symbol resembling an eye blinked and vanished._

_‒The rules are never as simple as they seem ‒said Iskar‒. Every choice you make in this city is written on these walls... but it can also be rewritten._

_‒You mean that... we can change the rules?_

_The Exegete smiled bitterly._

_‒We can try. And we must. But every attempt that opens a new door... closes others._

_Naevin fell silent. Iskar stepped away from the mural and headed for the tangled streets of the Ouroboros._

_‒Remember this ‒he added before disappearing into the gloom‒: Here it is not enough to be lucky... you have to know what price you are willing to pay for it._ »

The rules of Numinia draw on various existing systems, though they adapt them to the game's own identity and add integrations that define them as a unique system, suited to the essential nature of the universe that surrounds them: as a starting point, they combine the narrative freedom of two innovative systems: _PbtA_ (an acronym for _Powered by the Apocalypse_), created by Vincent and Meguey Baker in 2010 for the game _Apocalypse World_; and _CfB_ (an acronym for _Carved from Brindlewood_), created by Jason Cordova in 2020 for his game _Brindlewood Bay_. And, to a lesser extent, they draw on other systems such as _FitD_ (an acronym for Forged in the Dark), created in 2017 by John Harper for the game _Blades in the Dark_, from which it inherits the principle of fiction first; or the system of the game _Trophy_, created in 2018 by Jesse Ross for the game of the same name (later, _Trophy Dark_) and for its variant, _Trophy Gold_. Both systems are fused, in Numinia, with the structural precision of the D6 system. This balance seeks to ensure that the players' decisions are guided not only by chance or by the GD's will, but also by a shared and spontaneous creativity; and, fundamentally, it means that the narrative, as well as the development of the adventure, is built collaboratively between the players and the GD from the skeleton laid out in the module.

This fits the idea of what the living forces of Numinia represent: like a cosmos raised in the nebula of the Akashic Archives, the city moves by means of a gearwork subject to the dynamics of chaos and the unpredictable, where all the elements are mysteriously interconnected; thus, the movement of each of its parts sends tremors through the whole, giving the city a changing and unbridled morphology. Every choice is an echo in the fabric of the city, with the power to transform it.

Therefore, joint narrative and collaborative construction are the fundamental pillars of Numinia's game system, far more than dice rolls and the application of results and consequences. Nevertheless, the presence of such mechanics remains essential to supply the dose of chance required by a world whose ultimate designs escape the control of any will. And their action defines that ungovernable force we call destiny, circumstance, chance or causality. After all, in Numinia no one chooses what they must live through, only the way in which they wish to live it. Narrative is the energy that drives the engine of the game but, in the end, at one moment or another, the fate of the players will be decided by a dice roll. And this, ultimately, clearly reflects that mathematics of chaos which rules the gearwork. Every act of creation by the players is relevant, because it modifies and unsettles the world of Numinia; it is the latent, restless force of the city itself that answers, calling upon chaos in order to reorder itself enigmatically.

Since narrative is, then, the primordial catalyst of action in the game, and since it also works collaboratively, the Numinia system adopts two essential sources related to literary theory and narratology: Doležel's Heterocosmic, an epistemological theory that clears the ground for the creation of worlds in literature; and Propp's functions of the folktale, in which the author, from the standpoint of Russian formalism, sets out the different motifs that recur in classic tales. These two references, explained later in this chapter, will create the system of narrative creation and construction through which any adventure will unfold. From this adoption, which deeply integrates the dynamics of _CfB_, Numinia gives birth to a system of its own which we might call the _Numen_ system.

To this _Numen_ system we add, on the other hand, dynamics taken from the _PbtA_ system, such as the development of the adventure in scenes (which we also find in the _CfB_ system) and, from a more pragmatic angle, the carrying out of decisions through the activation of a series of specific manoeuvres, generically called _Movements_. These Movements clearly represent the freedom of action of the _PbtA_ games: unlike in traditional roleplaying games, in _PbtA_ games the players do not wait for the GD to describe every corner of the setting or every circumstance of a situation; instead, they improvise details, open doors and gaps, and take part in shaping the context. In Numinia there are two types of Movement Rolls: Action Rolls and Instrument Rolls. Each of the Actions is associated with one of the PC's characteristics, while Instrument Rolls are determined by the Competences and are made using some object (an artefact or relic); the way in which they manifest and are resolved is detailed later in this same chapter. Nevertheless, we can say in advance that the roll that resolves these movements is made with a specific number of D6; but, whereas the _PbtA_ system, which already incorporates the D6 system, stipulates a numerical roll, adding up the score of the dice thrown and using a difficulty table, the Numinia game relies on a system of successes. And if we said earlier that, in the end, the players' decisions depend on a dice roll, it is also true that in _PbtA_ games every roll, sometimes even successes, has consequences. And these consequences create new narratives and modify the story, something that, in its own way, the Numinia system also reflects.

## **Fragment 1: The Background**

As we have already said, the _Numen_ system is inspired by two great currents of roleplaying game design: _PbtA_ (_Powered by the Apocalypse_) and _CfB_ (_Carved from Brindlewood_).

Both systems were born in the spirit of open creation, with licences that allow their mechanics to be adapted, transformed and reinterpreted to bring new worlds to life. Their principles are neither rigid nor inflexible; they are designed to flow with the narrative, turning every scene into a living part of the story.

In a _PbtA_ system:

  - The focus is on the story: it is not just about rolling dice to resolve an action, but about how that result changes the course of the game.

  - Failure also moves the plot: every mistake introduces a new challenge, a complication or a twist in the story that enriches the play experience.

  - Actions trigger results: when a character attempts something relevant to the plot, they activate a movement, which requires a dice roll.

  - The GD reacts, not dictates: the system leaves room for improvisation and collaboration, allowing the players to determine the direction of the game.

The _PbtA_ system is based not on tactical precision or exhaustive tables, but on creating a fluid, narrative and meaningful experience.

In a _CfB_ system:

  - The story advances in scenes: each has a dramatic purpose ‒revealing a clue, showing a conflict‒ and ends when that purpose is fulfilled.

  - The players interpret the clues: the system offers no prewritten solution; the players interpret the clues, assembling their own puzzle.

  - The mystery is emergent: the narrative is built backwards; the answers arise from the connections the players draw between the fragments.

  - The GD accompanies, not leads: their role is to maintain the tone and guard the balance between the everyday and the strange, not to impose a closed plot.

  - The tone is atmospheric and progressive: conflicts escalate towards an emotional or symbolic climax, where the mystery is solved ‒or transformed‒ through revelation, not through simple victory.

The _CfB_ system turns investigation into a creative act: to discover is to interpret, and every interpretation alters the world.

With all this in mind, a very important consideration about the _Numen_ system must be borne in mind from the outset: in campaigns, the story is not a closed and entirely preconceived path; there is a starting point, a conflict, but neither the way it is resolved nor the story behind that conflict is defined. They are narrative compartments that will gradually take shape with the collaboration of all the players, through the same dynamics that Numinia inherits from _PbtA_ and _CfB_: answers to the GD's questions, mysteries emerging from the players' own resolutions, determinations and actions… The following points try to explain in detail how this collaborative construction of the story works.

To these two great influences is added the _FitD_ system, whose principle known as _fiction first_ - the fiction comes first - finds a direct correspondence with the philosophy of _Numen_. This principle establishes that the mechanics do not exist independently of what is happening in the fiction. Before determining which rule applies, which dice should be rolled or what consequences an action has, one must understand the narrative situation: what the character intends to achieve, how they are trying to do it, what circumstances favour or hinder them and what risks are present. The mechanics do not initially determine the fiction; it is the fiction that determines which mechanics make sense to apply.

This does not mean doing without rules, but putting them in their proper place. The dice come in when there is a relevant uncertainty, but the resolution must arise from what the characters are actually doing within the scene. The same action, formulated in the abstract, can therefore have very different consequences depending on the context in which it takes place. _Numen_ adopts this principle in an especially visible way in its conflict resolution system. A confrontation is not presented as a succession of independent attacks aimed at reducing a pool of points, but as a situation that must be observed, understood and interpreted <sup>8</sup>.

The fiction, therefore, comes before even the building of the dice pool. An additional die is not gained because a table automatically grants a modifier, but because the player has identified something significant within the scene and has explained how their character acts upon it. In a confrontation, each Anchor that is acted upon contributes 1D6 precisely because it has first been justified from within the fiction. This approach reinforces another principle shared with _FitD_: consequences should not leave the situation untouched. A significant failure must modify the conditions of the scene and force the players to react to a different reality.

In this way, the rules do not interrupt the narrative to resolve it from outside, but intervene in it to transform it. The fiction leads to the dice; the dice modify the fiction; and that determines what the next possibility for action will be.

And the other relevant influence among roleplaying games comes from the _Trophy_ system, especially from its way of understanding the relationship between fiction and dice rolls. In this kind of game, conversation, exploration, the characters' decisions and the building of the scene can unfold over long stretches without constantly resorting to mechanical resolution. The dice are reserved for those moments in which there is a truly significant uncertainty.

8 See later in this chapter the fragment devoted to the Confrontation System.

_Numen_ shares this conception. Not every action requires a roll. A good part of the game unfolds through roleplay, dialogue, description and decision-making, while the dice appear when there is a resistance, a risk or an uncertainty whose outcome can genuinely alter the course of the story. The system itself establishes that many actions are resolved solely through narration, without resorting to any random mechanic.

This economy of rolls is especially important in conflicts. The system avoids turning a confrontation into an accumulation of small, independent resolutions. When the roll finally happens, it does not necessarily represent a blow, an isolated manoeuvre or a few seconds of action: it may represent the outcome of an entire joint strategy.

For this reason, the confrontation system uses a single shared pool when several characters collaborate. Each of them can contribute to the scene through a different intervention, but all those efforts converge in a single resolution. The dice do not count successive actions: they narratively synthesise the way in which the group has understood and faced the conflict.

This influence leads to a fundamental idea of _Numen_: the importance of a roll does not depend on its frequency. On the contrary, the less routine the intervention of the dice, the greater their dramatic weight can be. The roll appears when something is truly at stake.

This conception also fits the narrative nature of Numinia itself. Its Bestiary establishes that creatures are not mere physical obstacles, but manifestations of the world's tensions, errors and dissonances; confronting them means reading them and understanding what they represent, and defeating them may depend more on that understanding than on doing them harm. The conflict resolution system carries this idea through to its ultimate mechanical consequences: first the problem is interpreted; afterwards, and only when uncertainty demands it, the dice are rolled.

**The synthesis of** **_Numen_**

_PbtA_, _CfB_, _FitD_ and _Trophy_ are not four sets of rules placed side by side. Numen takes certain principles from them and reorganises them around its own conception of collaborative narrative: from _PbtA_ it adopts the importance of consequences and of actions capable of transforming the fiction; from _CfB_, collective interpretation and the appearance of emergent solutions; from _FitD_, the _fiction first_ principle and the subordination of mechanics to the narrative situation; and from _Trophy_, an economy of resolution that reserves rolls for those moments in which there is a significant uncertainty.

Upon these influences, Numen builds its own mechanisms. The Heterocosmic allows the players to take part directly in the creation of the world, while the Events based on the Functions of the Tale and other elements make interpretation the basis for the narrative resolution of adventures. In confrontations, that same logic is expressed through elements of the fiction that must be interpreted and acted upon before they acquire mechanical value.

Thus, all these antecedents converge on one and the same principle: the mechanics exist to intervene in a narrative that is already in motion, not to replace it.

From all that has been set out, the motto of the Numen System could take this form:

_The fiction leads to the dice; the dice transform the fiction_.

## **Fragment 2: Phase structure**

The stories of Numinia unfold neither in a linear nor in a chaotic way. Like the tales that pass through the Veil themselves, each adventure tends to organise itself into a series of distinct narrative moments. These moments are not rigid acts or closed scenes, but states of the investigation and of the experience of the world.

To ease the pace of the game and the understanding of the conflict, each session or adventure usually unfolds through six phases. These phases are not watertight compartments: they can overlap, shorten or expand according to the needs of the story. However, they are an essential reference for both the GD and the players, since they reflect the natural movement of an investigation within Numinia.

The phases are as follows:

  - Presentation

  - Deliberation

  - Investigation

  - Reflection

  - Action

  - Resolution

Each of them represents a different relationship between the characters, the world and the mystery that runs through the story.

### **Presentation Phase**

During this phase the GD sets out the initial context of the conflict. It may be a rumour, an alteration in the Veil, a commission received by the characters or the appearance of an inexplicable phenomenon somewhere in the city.

In this phase the players' participation is relatively limited: their main role is to observe, listen and begin to interpret the signs that are presented. The GD describes the setting, introduces secondary characters and lays out the first elements of the situation. The idea, then, is for this phase to act as a literary manifestation, as though the players themselves were beginning to read a novel expressed through the GD's voice. This initial setup is what we shall call the Root of the campaign, a foundation that will serve as the original reference at all times.

However, even at this initial moment, the world of Numinia never presents itself as a completely closed reality. Through directed questions, the GD can invite the players to fill in details of the setting or of the narrative context.

Example:

_When you enter the archive hall, what object immediately catches your attention?_

The players' answers can be woven into the fiction, expanding the scene and enriching the shared world.

The presentation phase ends when the group has enough information to formulate a first interpretation of the conflict.

### **Deliberation Phase**

Once the problem has been set out, the players temporarily step outside the game and their role as PCs and begin to interpret what they have seen. It is, then, an extra-dramatic phase.

During the Deliberation Phase the players share their first hypotheses about the nature of the conflict. These hypotheses need not be correct or complete: their function is to guide the investigation and begin to give shape to the mystery. They are, in this way, first impressions, those sensations we receive from something or someone in a preliminary state.

But in Numinia hypotheses are not mere narrative conjectures. They often contain interpretative elements that may influence the later course of the story. All the ideas put forward by the players, as well as those elements they were invited to introduce in the Presentation Phase, will be incorporated into the reality of the fiction if they fit the logic of the world and the unfolding of the investigation. The GD will place all their effort and all their imagination at the service of creation so that this may be so.

Deliberation may be more or less brief. Its purpose is not to solve the mystery, but to set the characters' curiosity in motion, although it may well give rise to a heated debate that takes longer than expected. All of that is also part of the game. When the group decides to begin actively exploring the conflict and has obtained, or developed, enough paths to do so, the game enters the next phase.

### **Investigation Phase**

This is, generally, the longest phase of the adventure. In this phase the players take a far more prominent part than the GD, who will try to remain in the background, acting only as a guide and trying to let the players' own proposals create the narrative from the Root, channelling them and adapting them to that base whenever possible.

During the investigation the characters explore their surroundings, question witnesses, examine objects, cross the districts of the city and face the consequences of the anomaly under investigation. It is in this phase that Movement Rolls are triggered most often and where heterocosmic creations may also appear, when the players introduce new elements into the fiction. And, therefore, where the most Spontaneous Events are awakened. That is to say, the fact that this is the Investigation Phase, and not the Action Phase, does not mean there are no moves and actions on the part of the PCs. There is simply no specific focus on which to act yet.

Throughout the investigation the characters discover Pieces of Conflict: clues, leads, fragments of information or situations that reveal aspects of the mystery.

For the story to move forward, the characters must gather at least three Pieces of Conflict, although they will always have a better chance of bringing the campaign to a successful conclusion if they gather more, since each piece found will be worth, in the Closing Phase, 1D6 in the pool of the Prism Roll, which will serve to resolve the final step of the story. These pieces, therefore, do not constitute a definitive explanation of the problem, but they do provide enough material to confront it. In other words, they are not part of a closed story but, rather, pieces of a puzzle that has not yet been assembled, and which admits different interpretations.

During this phase it is also common for the players to introduce new narrative elements through their actions or through the GD's questions. The campaign takes shape through these interactions, and may drift noticeably from its original focus, the Root ‒which does not mean losing the Root, but establishing unexpected ramifications from it‒. In this sense, the campaign must be designed so that every element is ready to give rise to new narrative, not to close it <sup>9</sup> .

Additionally, in the Investigation Phase not only Pieces of Conflict are collected but also other kinds of information that help to give shape to the narrative, such as documents, audio notes, etc. Likewise, the Phase fosters the dynamic of Rumourology (detailed in Chapter 7, _Building the Adventure_ ).

When the PCs have come upon some clue or piece of evidence that clearly and precisely shows them how to face the conflict, where to head, they may move on to the Action Phase, provided they have gathered at least three Pieces of Conflict.

### **Reflection Phase**

Like the Deliberation Phase, it represents an extra-dramatic transition in the game. In it, the players once again talk over and debate everything they have investigated so far and establish a new hypothesis, now far more evolved and consolidated. In fact, the various hypotheses of this phase, unlike those of the Deliberation Phase, must reach common ground so as to determine, jointly, what their objective will be in the Action Phase. Perhaps it will not be to solve the mystery hidden behind the conflict, but to save an NPC ‒or to confront an NPC‒, to escape from some extreme situation… For their part, the GD will focus the entire narrative of the Action Phase on this common objective, setting aside everything that falls outside it. In this way, it will be understood that the PCs will never resolve a campaign completely, but only in one of its aspects. There will always remain unknowns, unanswered questions, open mysteries and circumstances that will be lost in the unknown.

### **Action Phase**

In this phase, the conflict becomes immediate and tangible.

The characters directly confront the elements that sustain the anomaly: an entity arisen from the Veil, a narrative artefact out of control, a faction manipulating the city's archives or any other force involved in the story.

As in the Investigation Phase, the PCs continue to hold in their hands the course and progress of the adventure, although the GD is an undisputed guide and moderator. Nevertheless, it must be designed so that the players feel a certain sense of lost control and dizzying pace, that the campaign is entering a phase of chaos where ‒almost‒ anything can happen.

9 See Chapter 7, _Building the Adventure_, to learn more about narrative dynamics.

The decisions taken during the investigation are now put to the test. Movement Rolls are frequent, consequences follow one another swiftly and the pace of the game usually intensifies.

The outcome of this phase does not always mean destroying or neutralising the conflict. In many cases the characters may choose to:

  - Contain the anomaly

  - Reinterpret it

  - Transform it

  - Integrate it in some way into the balance of Numinia.

When the conflict reaches a provisional resolution, the story enters its final phase. Whereas until now the players understood well how the passage from one phase to another came about, since the premise was clearly marked, in this case, and in keeping with the same atmosphere of chaos, the passage from the Action Phase to the Resolution Phase, though clearly stipulated by the GD, should feel more spontaneous to the players. They should move from one phase to the next without quite understanding how it happened, although the GD will always specify that the next phase has been entered.

And, in short, what determines the passage to the Resolution Phase is that the PCs have resolved enough events for the Prism Rolls, which make up the last phase, to take place. In other words, that they have reached a point in the plot that clearly calls for resolution, which, while quite clear to the GD, escapes the players' perception in a far more abstruse way.

### **Resolution Phase**

Once again, as in the Presentation Phase, the narrative dynamic will be in the hands of the GD, though with more latitude for the players, who will have to decide, resolve and act. As in the Investigation Phase, this phase also involves moves, actions and acts of creation on the part of the players. Nevertheless, the GD will guide them in a more controlled way towards the resolution of the campaign. This resolution will be subject to the intervention and decision of the PCs at different Operative Points.

Operative Points are key moments within the phase, at which the PCs will come up against one of the knots of the resolution <sup>10</sup> . To untie that knot, they will have to make a Prism Roll (at each point it may be freely made by whichever player the team prefers); to do so, they will use the Pieces of Conflict found throughout the whole campaign ‒a Piece of Conflict may be found even in the Action Phase‒, bearing in mind that each piece becomes 1D6 within the pool. This represents the PCs' acquired capacity to interpret the situation of the Operative Point. Success or failure (or critical failure; in this case they will mean the same) will not determine whether or not the PCs resolve one of the key points of the game, but will make the narrative travel down one path or another. Failure will only make things harder for the PCs.

10 For more information, see Chapter 7, _Building the Adventure_ .

By convention, the Resolution Phase comprises four Operative Points, through which the campaign should be resolved and closed. Nevertheless, the GD may reduce or increase the number of these Operative Points if they consider it appropriate to the character of the adventure, although these four points should correspond to each of the four logical operators used in the Heterocosmic Roll <sup>11</sup> .

Each of these points, moreover, should place the PCs in a complex situation, in which they must take an important decision that leads them to carry out some equally significant action. Nevertheless, the workings of this final resolution through rolls will vary slightly depending on the circumstances of each campaign.

The close does not always resolve every unknown. In Numinia it is common for some questions to remain open, like echoes of stories that have not yet finished being written.

This dynamic shapes the world of Numinia in one way or another. When the Resolution Phase concludes, something has changed. Some trace of that adventure will remain in Numinia, with its particular modifications upon the world of the city.

In any case, if you are approaching the adventure as GD, in Chapter 7, Building the Adventure, you will find more information on how to build each phase, how to encourage the narrative, how to gradually incorporate the players' contributions into the campaign, etc.

## **Fragment 3: Movement Rolls**

In the Numinia system, actions and challenges are resolved through different types of rolls that reflect the nature of the action undertaken. Each type of roll represents a different approach to interpreting the risk, the skill or the uncertainty surrounding a particular situation.

There are two main types of roll in the game:

  - Action Rolls: When the character triggers a specific Action that may unleash concrete narrative consequences.

  - Instrument Rolls: When the character uses a specific object, whether a relic ‒the generic name given to the various artefacts in Numinia‒ or a weapon of any kind.

Each of these rolls follows the logic of the D6 System, which the _Numen_ system adapts as follows:

To make Action or Instrument Rolls, the player throws as many six-sided dice (D6) as they have points in the corresponding aptitude (as detailed in the following points, each Action Roll depends on a Characteristic, physical or psychic, just as each Instrument Roll is made with a Competence).

These dice represent their pool. If the roll is an Action Roll, to these dice they will add the Threshold D6 pool for rolls that depend on Physical Characteristics and the Breath of the Veil D6 pool for rolls that depend on Psychic Characteristics. In some cases, they may add bonuses for special circumstances, which may translate into additional dice, provided the player coherently justifies their use. This dynamic is part of the creative spirit of Numinia, which seeks to encourage the narrative use of resources, promoting actions that are logical, ingenious and coherent with the setting.

11 See later in this same chapter for the rules of the Heterocosmic Roll.

Once the roll has been made, the results are counted as follows:

  - Each 6 rolled is a success.

  - Each 1 rolled is a failure.

The outcome of the roll is assessed thus:

  - If there is at least one 6 and at most a single 1, the action is a Normal Success, since, one for one, success wins.

  - But if there is more than one failure (1), each additional failure cancels out a success (6), cancelling itself out as well.

  - If in the final count there are three or more successes (6), it is considered a Total Success, with especially favourable consequences.

  - If in the final count there are neither successes (6) nor failures (1), it is a Failure without consequences.

  - If in the final count there are one or more failures (1) and no success (6), it is a Critical Failure, with negative consequences.

This resolution system not only determines the outcome of an action but opens the game up to narrative nuance: the GD can use the levels of success or failure to enrich the story with proportionate consequences, and the players can use their resources and narratives to modify, anticipate or endure those outcomes.

### **Action Rolls**

Action Rolls in Numinia represent those situations in which the character attempts to carry out a specific manoeuvre that involves a risk or a significant consequence. These actions are defined by the game system itself and are triggered when the character declares that they are performing an action, or when the GD understands that some player's initiative or move has triggered a move.

Each Action unleashes an outcome that may be:

Total Success: The character achieves their objective brilliantly, with an additional benefit.

Normal Success: The character achieves their objective, with positive consequences.

Failure: The character does not achieve their objective, which carries some minor consequence.

Critical Failure: A narrative complication arises that makes the situation even more difficult.

Unlike other rolls, Action Rolls are not limited to measuring the character's technical competence; they are designed to move the story forward. Even a failure generates consequences that enrich the narrative flow, driving the game towards new decisions, dilemmas and challenges.

Each Action is linked to a specific Characteristic. Thus, an Action Roll comprises the points of the associated Characteristic (as D6), plus the bonus of the Threshold or of the Breath of the Veil (if the Characteristic is physical or psychic respectively).

In Numinia, Action Rolls invite the players to take risks, to understand the consequences of their deeds and to see how their decisions shape the destiny that surrounds them.

There are sixteen actions, and each Characteristic has two of them associated with it:

**PERCEPTION:**

<u>Decode Surroundings</u>

When you carefully examine your surroundings to find anomalies, oddities or hidden particularities.

<u>Interpret</u>

When you analyse the visible elements of a situation (facts, gestures, objects, patterns) to discover the implicit meaning that connects them.

**CHARISMA:**

<u>Alter Perspective</u>

When you try to influence a character's perception, beliefs or decisions through speech.

<u>Negotiate</u>

When you handle a disagreement with another character by trying to adjust positions, expectations or desires until an acceptable agreement is reached.

**CONSTITUTION:**

<u>Anchor Will</u>

When you try to keep your composure, resist fear or hold on to your determination in the face of manipulation, danger or uncertainty.

<u>Physical Resistance</u>

When you try to withstand physical harm, such as poisons, diseases, dehydration or adverse environmental conditions.

**MOVEMENT:**

<u>Opaque Form</u>

When you try to move without being seen, act with stealth or go unnoticed in a compromising situation.

<u>Agility</u>

When you perform a physical manoeuvre that demands coordination, speed and bodily control to execute a dynamic movement with precision.

**STRENGTH:**

<u>Power</u>

When you try to exert physical force to strike, break or alter something through brute strength.

<u>Bodily Dominion</u>

When you use your strength to physically control something or someone through restraint, grip, containment or sustained pressure.

**SIZE:**

<u>Impose Presence</u>

When you try to influence someone's behaviour or dominate a setting through your sheer physical presence.

<u>Human Barrier</u>

When you use your bulk to block the way, protect others or control a space through the mere occupation of your body.

**WISDOM:**

<u>Unravel Soul</u>

When you try to understand someone's emotion, intention or purpose through their body language, their words or their reactions.

<u>Mystic Power</u>

**INTELLIGENCE:**

<u>Plot Strategy</u>

When you try to analyse a complex situation to find the best options, routes or strategies.

When you investigate a matter to gather relevant information through rumours, testimonies, records or the following of leads.

It must be borne in mind that, although all these Basic Actions specify which elements of the context (NPCs, objects, marks in the surroundings) trigger each action, the integration of those elements is left to the GD at the moment a player declares their wish to carry out a game action, within the narrative.

### **Instrument Rolls**

Instrument Rolls in Numinia represent the use of artefacts, which in the world of Numinia are called Relics, and whose handling depends on specialised skills that require technical knowledge, training or prior experience; these abilities are gathered in the nine Competences, whose character is woven into the imaginary of Numinia <sup>12</sup> . Instrument Rolls are used when the character attempts to resolve a situation by resorting to one of their Relics, for which they must make a roll with the D6 pool of the Competence needed to use that Relic. Let us remember that Competences in Numinia are associated with Guild, Faction and Species. Those Competences that the PC does not have associated with any of these three attributes are considered not to be part of their knowledge, and cannot be used by them. As for those the PC does have associated, it does not matter if the player has left them at 0, for they are Competences whose level can be raised at any given moment with Prestige Points.

Unlike Action Rolls, which are triggered by a specific manoeuvre defined in the system, Competence Rolls depend entirely on the narrative context: the properties of Relics in Numinia are open enough to be interpretable in different situations. It will be the player's imagination that enables the application of the Relic in each situation, although the GD's task will be to judge wisely whether the proposed use answers to logic and coherence.

As with Action Rolls, the result of an Instrument Roll may be:

Total Success: The character achieves their goal brilliantly, with an additional benefit.

Normal Success: The character simply achieves their goal.

Failure: The character simply fails to achieve their goal.

Critical Failure: A narrative complication arises that makes the situation even more difficult.

## **Fragment 4: Heterocosmic Roll**

When we approach a narrative, we tend to think of it as a story that takes place «in our world», as in _The Three Musketeers_, _Lord of the Flies_ and _I, Claudius_ . Nevertheless, sometimes we are taken to our world, but one into which some element at odds with reality (fantastic or supernatural) has been inserted, as in _Dracula_, _A Christmas Carol_ and _Journeys to the Other World_ . On many other occasions, the narrative will take us to contexts far removed from our reality, fantastic, futuristic or uchronic, as happens with _The Lord of the Rings_, _Brave New World_ and _Foundation_ . It is from this last perspective that the philologist and literary theorist Lubomír Doležel proposed a view that nonetheless structures and explains all three types of narrative: each tale creates an alternative cosmos, a possible world that exists with its own laws, which may differ from those of everyday reality, whether categorically or subtly. In truth, the insertion of the poetic function into the text already creates a very fine layer of fantasy in any tale, however much it may be apparently set and contextualised in our everyday world.

Doležel called this theory the Heterocosmic: the science of fictional worlds. From this perspective, reading, telling or playing a story is not simply to embellish reality, but to open a parallel universe with its own order of possibilities, norms, knowledge and values. In short, with its own logic.

Doležel's great contribution was to show that narrative does not stand in a void: every alternative cosmos answers to a dimension of coherence, even if this differs from the one that articulates our own reality. A tale may allow the dead to speak, time to run backwards or moral laws to be inverted, but these exceptions are not arbitrary, anarchic or meaningless merely because they are fantastic: they form part of the fabric that defines that universe. This principle refutes the «anything goes» by which the context of a fantastic worldview is sometimes judged.

Applied to roleplaying, this approach invites us to think of each game session as the joint creation of a possible world. When narrator and players contribute elements, they are not merely inventing details: they are raising a cosmos with its own internal grammar, where every action, every discovery and every value is governed by a system that adapts to the logic of the tale.

### **A brief portrait**

Lubomír Doležel (1922–2017), doctor of Philology and educator, was a Czech-Canadian literary theorist, a pioneer in applying the logic of possible worlds to narrative analysis. His most influential work, _Heterocosmica: Fiction and Possible Worlds_ (1998), changed the way we understand fiction: not as a mere reflection of the real world, but as the creation of alternative cosmoi with their own rules of existence.

### **Overview of the Heterocosmic**

The Heterocosmic holds that every narrative opens a possible world. Each story is a parallel cosmos, in which fantasy and possibility themselves enable that world to operate with internal laws, where actions, characters and values acquire a coherence of their own. Unlike the real world, which we perceive as unique, fictions multiply realities: each tale is an ontological laboratory.

Doležel distinguishes several dimensions to describe these worlds:

  - What can or cannot happen.

  - What must or must not happen.

  - What is known and what is unknown.

  - What is judged to be good or bad.

These dimensions are articulated through the logical operators, which allow us to explore how a narrative universe is built and regulated.

### **The logical operators**

**1.** **Alethic operators – The horizon of the possible**

The alethic operators (from the Greek _alétheia_, «truth») determine what is possible, impossible, necessary or contingent in a narrative cosmos. They are not limited to a particular detail: they are the ontological framework of the world.

In our universe, for example, flying without artifice is impossible; in an alternative cosmos it may be an everyday fact and necessary for life.

In Numinia, each district shapes its own alethics: in _Sycamore_, it is possible for a painting to sing; in _Ouroboros_, for a door to open when a riddle is answered. What would be absurd in our world is natural law there.

**2.** **Deontic operators – The cartography of the norm**

These operators (from the Greek _déon -ontos_, «that which must be») chart what is permitted, forbidden and obligatory in a cosmos. They are norms that permeate the fabric of the world. In our world, killing is forbidden; in another, it may be an initiation rite.

In Numinia, the deontics vary according to faction: for the Hermeticists, studying is a duty; for the Heirs of Eleusis, playing is a sacred mandate. The deontic does not regulate actions alone, but the very meaning of existence in that cosmos.

**3.** **Epistemic operators – The limits of knowledge**

The epistemic operators (from the Greek _episteme_, «knowledge») determine what is known, unknown and unknowable in the narrative universe. It is not only a matter of what a character is ignorant of, but of what the entire cosmos admits as knowledge.

In our world, we know that the universe was born of the Big Bang; in an alternative cosmos, perhaps its origin is an unanswered enigma, or electricity has not yet been discovered.

In Numinia, epistemology is always fragmented: what in _Vitruvian_ is known and taught in academies, in _Ouroboros_ is a secret disguised as a riddle. Knowledge is never neutral: what is known and what is hidden defines how life is lived in the city.

**4.** **Axiological operators – The scales of values**

The axiological operators (from the Greek _axios_, «valuable»; and _logos_, «study») establish the fundamental morality of a cosmos: what is judged good or bad, noble or vile. They describe not only concrete acts, but the system of values that runs through the whole narrative world.

In our world, infidelity is censured; in another, it may be considered an honourable practice. Gold, valuable in our world, may be trivial in another.

In Numinia, values are contested: for the Stellar Circle, order is the supreme good; for the Neo-Atlantists, anarchic creativity is a virtue. Thus, the same action ‒toppling a data tower‒ may be seen as heroism or as crime, depending on the axiological framework in which it is inscribed.

### **Heterocosmic Roll**

**What it is**

The game of Numinia allows a particularity within the framework of the roleplaying world: players have the capacity to build part of the context, imagining and creating objects within it; always, that is, applying a narrative coherence that fits the imaginary of the game and the logic of the story and of the moment. Nevertheless, the world of Numinia is enveloped by the force of the Akasha, which is the Veil. This force causes all the elements of the cosmos that makes up the city to be interconnected, sometimes in ways that even escape the understanding of its citizens: only chaos and its mysterious structure of order can explain all the deep interconnections.

Thus, an act of creation, a movement in one direction, an event, provoke unexpected reactions in the city. The appearance of an ancient bas-relief unleashes a sandstorm in the Kallis Crater; the destruction of some magic stones makes a horde of Formrenders appear in the Neuma Subvale… Everything that moves in Numinia, in one direction or another, has unexpected consequences, for, just as the city is open to being expanded in form and concept, it also reveals its unfinished character.

The Heterocosmic Roll is triggered when a player imagines and materialises something new in the scene (an object, a feature of the surroundings, a contact, a shortcut, a ritual…). That gesture sets the machinery of Numinia in motion: the world reacts and reconfigures its laws around the creation.

Principle: if you name it, it can exist; if it exists, it will have a price.

However, in this respect a warning must be given about the indiscriminate use of the _deus ex machina_, a narrative device historically used to unblock a seemingly unsolvable situation through the inclusion of some sudden element of which there had been no mention until then. In the literary field, this licence is considered a poor and mediocre device, a view that the world of Numinia and the GD must share and forbid.

**How it is rolled**

Whenever a player carries out a creation in order to resolve, accelerate or unblock the current situation, the Heterocosmic Roll is triggered. It requires no prior permission: the right to create is part of the system. But it must be borne in mind that stirring the machinery of Numinia too much can provoke serious disturbances. For this reason, and to control this dynamic, there is the Resonance Marker. Each roll adds 1 node to the Resonance Marker, which, of course, is collective (that is, it accumulates the nodes generated by all the players); on reaching 4, it triggers an Event Roll (see later in this same chapter the section on Propp's functions of the folktale) and resets to 0.

The roll will yield a result that will indicate not only the degree of success of the creation, but also some characteristics of the created object, which will be expressed following the logical operators of Doležel's Heterocosmic.

The player says what appears and for what purpose. For example, a player facing a computer artefact with encrypted source code might declare: «I find a cryptography manual that guides me through the code». They must provide a brief diegetic anchoring, that is, a sentence of plausibility: where it was and why it is believable in Numinia and in the context of the story.

Next, they make their roll with 2D6, abiding by the results of this table (which have been drawn up considering the probabilities of each result, taking into account their possible combinations):

|RESULT|EFFECT|OPERATORS|POLARITY|
|---|---|---|---|
|2 or 12|Moderate Success|2 operators|1 positive / 1 negative|
|3, 4, 10 and 11|Normal Success|3 operators|2 positive / 1 negative|
|5, 6, 8 and 9|Total Success|4 operators|3 positive / 1 negative|
|7|Critical Success|4 operators|4 positive|

For all results, the book will possess a series of characteristics whose number will depend on the type of success, as shown in the table. These characteristics will conform to the logical operators of the Heterocosmic, and the types will be chosen by the player who triggered the roll (except in Total and Critical Successes, in which all four types appear). As for their polarity, it will be the GD who decides which is positive and which negative. But it will be the other players who decide what the characteristics in question are.

Example: _suppose the player obtains a 10 on their roll. They are entitled to three characteristics, subject to three of the operators; given the nature of the object and the context, the player chooses the alethic, deontic and epistemic operators. One of them must be negative and the other two positive; the GD decides that the alethic and the epistemic are positive, while the deontic is negative. The GD then designates three players so that each invents one characteristic. The first player, taking as reference the alethic operator (positive), which indicates what is or is not possible, decides that the book can be read at a speed 10 times greater than normal; the second player, taking as reference the deontic operator (negative), which alludes to what is or is not permitted, states that the book belongs to a series of manuals whose reading is permitted only to engineer alchemists (this does not mean the player cannot read it if they are not an engineer alchemist, but that in doing so they will be breaking a serious rule of the district's laws, and could be punished for it); finally, the third player, taking as reference the epistemic operator (positive), which concerns what is known and what is not known, invents that the book conceals a block of information from the Dark Age, unknown to the inhabitants of the Golden Age (the present one), which could offer great advantages in the future._

As can be seen, this roll encourages collective creation and places part of the shaping of the adventure in the players' hands, since the story will change and transform according to what they invent.

But a clarification is needed, since this roll, which greatly enriches the _Numen_ game system, may prove somewhat confusing if not properly understood. Each operator determines a specific dimension of the created reality: the alethic operator establishes what is possible, impossible, necessary or contingent; the deontic, what is permitted, forbidden or obligatory; the epistemic, what is known, unknown or remains hidden; and the axiological, what is considered valuable, harmful, desirable or objectionable. These categories describe how what has been created exists within the cosmos of Numinia. The positive-negative polarity obtained through the Heterocosmic Roll operates on a different level: it determines how that characteristic affects the narrative situation and, in particular, the PCs. Therefore, a positive operator does not represent an affirmation, nor a negative one a negation. A positive alethic result does not mean «this is possible», just as a negative one does not mean «this is not possible». In both cases, the operator keeps behaving in the same way because it is defining the conditions of possibility of what has been created; by applying the positive-negative polarity to it we merely establish whether those conditions turn out to be favourable or unfavourable for the PCs in the present situation.

For example, an object might possess as an alethic characteristic that it can only work during the night. That limitation belongs to its nature: it establishes under what conditions its functioning is possible. Its polarity, however, will depend on the narrative situation. If the PCs need to use it at night, the characteristic may prove positive; if they need to employ it in broad daylight, it may prove negative. The property of the object does not change: what changes is its effect on the story.

In short, the operators answer the question «what is this reality like and how does it work?», while the positive-negative polarity answers «how does that reality affect the PCs and the situation they are living through?». One defines the logical behaviour of the object; the other determines in what way the Veil turns its existence into an advantage or a complication.

Likewise, it must be specified that when we speak of a _created object_ we do not necessarily mean a physical object, since the Heterocosmic can perfectly well be applied to an abstract object: a solar eclipse forms; a sandstorm breaks out; an NPC is, in reality, a ghost…

As can be seen, this mechanic works in an apparently complex way, but once in play it becomes much clearer. It does, however, require a good dose of imagination not only from the player who proposed the created object, but also from the rest, who must apply positive or negative impacts on the story to that object. To help with this imaginative exercise, and so that the nature of the operators becomes more understandable, we provide a table reflecting the way in which Doležel's Heterocosmic is applied in the game, with a more functional translation and a more direct description:

|OPERATOR|CONCEPT|FUNCTIONAL TRANSLATION|DESCRIPTION|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Alethic**|What is and is not<br>possible|What allows this to happen|_Possibility and_<br>_conditions_|
|**Deontic**|What must or<br>must not be done|What rules it triggers or breaks|_Norms and_<br>_obligations_|
|**Epistemic**|What is known or<br>not known|What it reveals or hides|_Knowledge and_<br>_information_|
|**Axiological**|What has or does not<br>have value|Why it matters|_Value and importance_|

Let us now look at two examples of the way the system works, to illustrate what has been set out more clearly:

#### **EXAMPLE 1**

The PC, who is a Game Master, visits the House of Riddles. The player invents that there is something there that leads them to continue their investigation at the Hyperborean Tavern; a talking mask fixed to one of the stone walls reveals to them that they will find a clue in the tavern.

**Alethic (Possibility and conditions)**

  - Positive: _There is a living riddle in the tavern that additionally offers two rumours besides the clue found._

  - Negative: _They will only have access to the clue in the Hyperborean Tavern if they visit it in a simulation that holds anomalies and dangers._

Plain translation: What makes this connection possible?

**Deontic (Norms and obligations)**

  - Positive: _The Hyperborean Tavern is a place widely visited by Game Masters; any roll there has a +1D6 modifier._

  - Negative: _They only have access to the clue if they agree to take part in a game at the Tavern (a wager, a challenge, a social test)._

Translation: What does it oblige you to do, or what rule does it impose?

**Epistemic (Knowledge and information)**

  - Positive: _The Tavern is a vortex of experiences relating to the adventure, and holds hidden knowledge that may be revealed._

  - Negative: _The clue is ambiguous: you do not know whether it is a place, a person or an event in the Tavern._

Translation: What do you know now and what remains uncertain?

**Axiological (Value and importance)**

  - Positive: _Going to the Tavern may grant Prestige Points for what is learned there._

  - Negative: _The Tavern is associated with debts, favours or social dangers._

Translation: Why is this useful and why might it go wrong?

#### **EXAMPLE 2**

The player's PC has an interview with a lady of high society; it has previously been established that in the city of Numinia there is a cameo that marks out certain individuals of a particular elite, who form a lodge and, in this way, know some restricted secrets of the Veil through a collective-memory protocol called «Elizabeth». This cameo thus works as an emblem. The player invents that the lady is wearing that cameo.

**Alethic (Possibility and conditions)**

  - Positive:
_The cameo reveals its secrets telepathically to its bearer, so that anyone who wears it could gain access to some of these secrets._

  - Negative:
_The cameo can be forged, or its meaning has recently changed, taking on different duties._

Translation: What makes this object what it is?

**Deontic (Norms and obligations)**

  - Positive:
_Wearing it grants access or legitimacy before certain circles, so that it works as an object of prestige._

  - Negative:
_Wearing it in public and in plain sight means breaking the rules of the Scions of Elizabeth, the lodge that guards the secrets of the Veil._

Translation: What rules does it activate or put at risk?

**Epistemic (Sign / Mystery)**

  - Positive:
_Elizabeth is a concept that lives in the unconscious of every citizen; seeing the cameo in person awakens that knowledge._

  - Negative:
_The revelations obtained from Elizabeth through the cameo can cause altered states of consciousness, even lead to madness._

Translation: What information does it unlock, and what does it conceal?

**Axiological (Value / Risk)**

  - Positive:
_It is a direct opportunity to access hidden knowledge, so it can be sold for a very high price on the black market._

  - Negative:
_Getting close to the cameo or to whoever wears it can draw the PC into dangerous conspiracies._

Translation: Why is this valuable, and what price might it have?

## **Fragment 5: Event Roll**

When the Russian formalist Vladimir Propp published _Morphology of the Folktale_ in 1928, he put on the table an idea as powerful as it was simple: stories ‒at least those of the oral tradition, the wonder tales‒ are not built from specific characters, but from narrative functions. What matters is not «who» acts, but «what» function that action fulfils in the development of the tale.

Propp's genius lay in showing that, behind the multiplicity of traditional tales, there was a shared narrative grammar: a limited number of functions that appeared again and again, in a relatively stable order, as though they were the pieces of a gearwork. Thus, the hero may be an unknown prince or a young peasant girl, but the function they fulfil when receiving a trial, overcoming an obstacle or returning transformed is always the same.

For us, designers of games and players of Numinia, this discovery is enormously valuable: it reminds us that narrative is not arbitrary, but answers to functional needs. Every twist of the plot is born because the tale demands it: if someone sets off on a journey, someone must say goodbye; if an obstacle arises, there must be an attempt to overcome it; if an object is lost, there will be a search. In the tale, action and context come together to generate the narrative function.

### **A brief portrait**

Vladimir Propp (1895–1970), anthropologist and linguist, a member of the school of Russian formalism, analysed folk tales in depth and discovered that, beneath the diversity of stories, there was a recurring structure. In _Morphology of the Folktale_ (1928) he identified 31 narrative functions, archetypal steps repeated again and again in the oral tradition: absence, interdiction, violation, the trial, the reward, and so on.

The decisive point was the idea that functions are more important than specific characters: what matters is not who carries out an action, but what place that action occupies in the logic of the tale. A peasant, a prince or a little girl can play the role of hero, provided they pass through the same functions.

Propp's work marked a turning point: it inspired Lévi-Strauss's structuralism and modern narratology (Todorov, Greimas), and it remains a reference for screenwriters, novelists and game designers. His gaze reminds us that narrative is not a mere capricious flow, but a web of functional needs that are activated in sequence.

### **Function as narrative need**

What Propp revealed is that narrative elements are not mere ornaments: they fulfil a function. A function appears when the tale needs it, just as a sign arises from the relationship between expression and content. If a dragon appears in a story, it is not only there to embellish it: it is because the tale requires a trial, an obstacle, a threshold.

Functions, therefore, are answers to needs:

  - If there is a hero, the tale needs a mission

  - If there is a magical object, it is because the tale demands a means of fulfilling that mission

  - If there is a villain, it is because the function of confrontation must be activated

### **Propp's 31 functions**

It is not necessary to memorise all 31, but it is necessary to understand that each one represents a narrative archetype. Not all of them appear in every tale, nor always in the same order, but together they form the «genetic code» of folk narratives. This is the list of the 31 functions identified by Propp:

1. Absence: someone dear is missing or the hero is far away; an initial void opens.

2. Interdiction (prohibition): a rule or taboo is laid upon the hero.

3. Violation: the interdiction is broken, deliberately or by accident.

4. Reconnaissance by the villain (interrogation): the villain spies on/probes their victims.

5. Delivery (information): the villain obtains information about the victim/target.

6. Trickery: the villain disguises themselves/deceives in order to get close.

7. Complicity: the victim unwittingly cooperates with the villain.

8. Villainy or lack: harm is done, or a lack arises that must be resolved.

9. Mediation: news of the villainy arrives; the mission is formulated.

10. Beginning counteraction: the hero agrees to act.

11. Departure: the hero leaves home/the initial state.

12. Test by the donor: an agent tests the hero.

13. Hero's reaction: passes/fails the test.

14. Acquisition of the magical object: receives help/an artefact/a power.

15. Transference: journey towards the place of the action.

16. Struggle: direct confrontation with the antagonist.

17. Branding: the hero is marked (scar, emblem, token).

18. Victory: defeat of the villain or overcoming of the obstacle.

19. Liquidation: the harm is repaired or the lack is filled.

20. Return: coming back from the space of the trial.

21. Pursuit: the hero is pursued.

22. Rescue: someone or something comes to the hero's aid.

23. Unrecognised arrival: an unrecognised, disguised return.

24. Unfounded claims: the false hero claims the merit.

25. Difficult task: an extraordinary trial is imposed.

26. Solution: the hero accomplishes the task.

27. Recognition: the hero is identified as such.

28. Exposure: the false hero/villain is revealed.

29. Transfiguration: change of state/form/dignity of the hero.

30. Punishment: sanction for the villain/false hero.

31. Wedding/ascension: final reward; restorative closure.

Examples of recurring functions:

  - Interdiction → violation: the hero receives a rule and breaks it (Orpheus must not look back, but he does).

  - Trial → reward: overcoming an obstacle grants a gift (Heracles completes his labours, Odysseus wins his homecoming).

  - Trickery → discovery: someone tries to deceive, someone uncovers the truth.

### **Event Roll**

**What it is**

The Event Roll is the second of Numinia's Narrative Rolls; the first is the Heterocosmic Roll.

It has already been explained that an Event Roll is triggered when the Resonance Marker of the Heterocosmic fills up (each point on the marker is equivalent to one Heterocosmic Roll). This is justified by the same interconnection that drives spontaneous creations: every relevant action in Numinia has a consequence; it creates an unexpected movement that, mysteriously, connects back to the first action. It is the city's way of reordering itself.

**How it is rolled**

The Event Roll does not measure the success or failure of the action, since we are not speaking of an action that requires a dice roll; rather, it expresses what kind of reaction the action itself provokes. To do so, 1D20 is rolled and the table of events (functions) set out below is consulted:

|D20|Function (Event)|Activation guideline at the table|
|---|---|---|
|1|Absence|Someone or something disappears.|
|2|Interdiction|A taboo or rule appears.|
|3|Violation|Something or someone crosses the line.|
|4|Villainy|Someone commits a crime.|
|5|Mediation|A call to change occurs.|
|6|Departure|Someone leaves.|
|7|Trial|Someone is subjected to a trial.|
|8|Reaction|A decision that changes course.|
|9|Magic|A magical object appears.|
|10|Transference|There is a change of plane.|
|11|Struggle|A confrontation takes place.|
|12|Branding|Something or someone leaves a deep mark.|
|13|Rescue|Unexpected help arrives.|
|14|Task|Someone is put to the test.|
|15|Solution|Someone must accomplish some task.|
|16|Recognition|Some secret identity is revealed.|
|17|Transfiguration|A change of state occurs.|
|18|Punishment|Someone receives a punishment.|
|19|Liquidation|A fracture is restored.|
|20|Ascension|Someone receives a reward.|

This table has been drawn up especially for the game, gathering the 20 most relevant of the 31 functions identified by Propp and turning them into playable Events.

Once the event has been determined, it is the GD who introduces it into the story, creating a narrative occurrence. Occasionally, the GD may put a question to each player about this event, such as:

What trait does this new event have that makes it unsettling?

What gesture can be seen in the NPC who has come into play as a result of the new event?

It is important to point out that this event does not have to appear in the same scene in which the roll was made. It may appear later, in another context. It will be the GD who decides when and how the event appears.

Example: _Investigating some ruins, one of the PCs notices that behind a broken column there is an old artefact for measuring resonances, which may help them find an anomaly hidden in the place. The player triggers a Heterocosmic Roll by creating this object and, with it, fills the Resonance Marker, which already stood at 3 points. So an Event Roll is triggered. A player is chosen to roll the D20, and gets a 14: Task (Someone is put to the test). The GD announces: «So, you have found a resonance meter, but for it to work, its core requires three divergent seals». Then the GD puts a question to each player: «What is there in the surroundings that indicates where one of the seals might be found?», «What do the divergent seals look like in your memory?», «Which of the PCs do you think keeps a divergent seal in their backpack?» and «Who is best suited to cast a spell that summons an entity that creates divergent seals?»_

As can be seen, each of these questions generates new narratives in the players' imagination. They must not only answer the question; it also leads them to invent additional details: an object that offers a clue as to where a seal is hidden, perhaps because it has a riddle engraved on it; a situation in the past in which the PC came across a divergent seal, perhaps on another mission; a reason why one of the PCs keeps a divergent seal in their backpack, and a situation in which the PC answering the question saw that seal inside the backpack; an entity that creates divergent seals, of unknown nature, whether benevolent or wild, and whether it can be kept under control… And, more importantly, it leads them to ask themselves a question they will have to resolve together: what on earth, exactly, is a divergent seal?

But this roll is also subject to the accumulation of events. Just as the Heterocosmic Roll is subject to a Resonance Marker, Event Rolls advance the Plot Clock, which likewise has a cycle of four hours. When the clock reaches the fourth hour, an Imbalance of the Gearwork occurs and the clock resets. Of course, the Plot Clock, like the Resonance Marker, is shared by all the players, accumulating the events generated by all of them.

### **Imbalance of the Gearwork**

When the Plot Clock reaches its fourth hour, the world stops for an instant. The lights of Numinia flicker, signs fall into disorder, the streets seem to remember other shapes. That tremor is neither chance nor punishment: it is the Imbalance of the Gearwork, the way in which the city readjusts its inner laws in order to go on existing.

Numinia, like every living organism, does not tolerate stagnation. Every Heterocosmic Roll, every Event, every creative or destructive impulse of the players alters, however slightly, the invisible flow of the Veil. When that flow reaches a point of saturation, the Gearwork ‒the very structure that sustains reality, which is subject to the force of the Prism‒ is forced to rebalance itself. In other words: every time the players force the logic of the world, the world responds by reorganising itself.

The Imbalance of the Gearwork is, then, a systemic reaction, a violent breath of Numinia. It represents the moment when the cosmos needs to adjust itself. It is not a punishment for the players, but an inevitable consequence of their own freedom. In Numinia, every act of creation or transgression leaves a trace; and when those traces accumulate, the city needs to redistribute its meaning.

Each Event Roll advances the Plot Clock by one hour. This clock, which is collective for all the players, marks the pulse of the narrative world. When it reaches the fourth hour, the Gearwork enters maximum tension and the Imbalance is triggered automatically. There is no way to avoid it or stop it; like the tide, it happens because it must happen.

The GD then announces: _The Gearwork comes out of true. Numinia changes its rhythm._

When the Imbalance takes place, the space around the PCs undergoes a collapse, which does not destroy the world; it _folds_ it. A scene opens set in the past or future of Numinia, or in an alternative echo of the same place.

  - Dissolution: the GD announces that _«The Veil folds»._ The current space blurs (lights, sounds or codes are distorted).

  - Translation: the group finds itself in a scene from the past or the future, or in the same place under different conditions (another time, another story).

  - Narrative purpose: the scene must reveal something hidden in the story; an origin, a future cause, an alternative meaning of the conflict.

  - Duration: it is played as a self-contained one-scene mini-adventure.

  - Refitting: when the scene closes, the characters return to the present, with altered behaviours, intuitions or perceptions.

The Veil «heals», and the Plot Clock resets to 0. The new perspective recontextualises the adventure, with some new and relevant information that is included in the story.

In short: the general collapse becomes an ontological leap, in which the players do not lose the story, but discover that they were inside one of its folds.

## **Fragment 6: Confrontation System**

### **Confrontation as conflict**

In Numinia, a confrontation is not a category separate from other conflicts. There is no distinct logic for «fighting» and another for investigating, fleeing, persuading, crossing an anomaly or resolving an obstacle. Every conflict is born from the same question:

What prevents the character from achieving what they intend?

So this system could just as well be called the Conflict Resolution System.

The difference is that, in a confrontation, the obstacle has agency. It is not a locked door, a rift in the Veil or a collapsed structure. It is a contender: an active presence that responds, moves, presses, threatens and transforms the situation.

A contender can be a creature, an NPC, a collective entity or even a group of attackers operating as a single threat. What matters is not its form, but its function within the conflict: it opposes the characters and forces them to interpret what sustains it, what protects it, what makes it dangerous and how it can be overcome.

That is why confrontations in Numinia are not resolved by making successive attack rolls to wear down hit points. They are resolved like a narrative puzzle: by declaring an objective and resolving the points of the conflict.

The player does not simply ask: How do I hurt it? But rather: What is happening here? What sustains this creature? What must I understand, alter or exploit in order to impose my action? And, above all: What do I want to achieve with this confrontation?

### **Contenders and anchors**

Every contender is defined by a series of _anchors._

An anchor is a piece of the confrontation's puzzle. It is a condition that sustains the contender's presence, capacity for action or threat.

  - An anchor can represent many things:

  - An essential quality of the creature

  - A pattern of behaviour

  - A symbolic protection

  - A bond with another plane

  - A form of movement

  - A positional advantage

  - A circumstance of the environment

An anchor is not necessarily a physical part of the enemy. In Numinia, presences are not bodies of flesh and blood. They are, essentially, etheric, metaphysical forms, entities sustained by bonds, echoes, distortions or patterns. That is why an anchor should not be understood as a piece of «armour», but as that which enables the contender to operate as a threat.

Put simply: an anchor is something the PC must take into account for their action to make sense and to be able to face the obstacle.

### **Fixed anchors and variable anchors**

Contender sheets distinguish between fixed anchors and variable anchors.

**Fixed anchors**

Fixed anchors express the contender's identity. They are those conditions that always appear, in one form or another, because they belong to its nature.

For example, an Echo Predator might always have an anchor related to its manifestation: it does not fully belong to Numinia and needs a condition that sustains it in the scene (a summoning, a rift); or an anchor inherent to its nature, such as its shifting movement, vanishing at one point and appearing at another.

But these anchors do not turn every encounter with that creature into the same fight. They simply ensure that the contender stays coherent.

**Variable anchors**

Variable anchors depend on the scene. They change according to the place, the context, the manner of appearance, the environment, the summoning, the corruption present or the narrative situation. They are the most numerous.

The same Echo Predator may manifest once in a burning archive and another time in a soundless hall. Its nature remains recognisable, but the puzzle changes. This prevents players from «learning the solution» to a creature and repeating it mechanically.

The player must not memorise how a creature is defeated. They must learn to interpret how it manifests in that encounter.

### **The GD's description**

The GD does not list the anchors when presenting a confrontation. They do not say: «The creature has these four anchors.»

Instead, they describe the scene, the creature and its behaviour in a way that is clear, sufficient and playable. The description must allow the players to understand what they are facing, but without turning the encounter into a list of instructions.

A good description should show:

  - What the contender looks like

  - How it moves

  - How it threatens

  - What it alters in its surroundings

  - What seems to sustain it

  - What behaviours repeat themselves

  - What elements seem strange, insistent or significant

The GD does not withhold information to make the confrontation cryptic. They present it as fiction. The players must interpret that fiction.

Because the anchor is not announced. It is suggested and demonstrated.

### **Infer, interpret and intervene**

Before a confrontation, the players receive the description and interpret it. They may infer anchors that might be foreseen in the contender's sheet, or interpret emergent anchors from the elements present in the scene.

This does not mean they can invent just anything.

A valid interpretation must meet three conditions:

  - It must rest on something that has been described

  - It must explain an observable behaviour

  - It must not contradict what has been established in the fiction

If the GD describes that the creature always reappears where the light is weakest, the players may interpret: that the darkness sustains it; that the light interferes with its manifestation; that its position depends on the shadows. All of those readings may be valid if they hold up within the scene.

What matters is this: anchors are neither discovered as closed answers nor freely invented. They are interpreted and brought into play.

### **Intervening an anchor**

When a player decides to act upon a specific interpretation of the conflict, they are intervening an anchor. Intervening an anchor does not necessarily mean resolving it. The definitive resolution will be known afterwards, with the roll. This is important.

Before rolling, the player does not know whether their action will eliminate the anchor, exploit it, get around it or only partially affect it. What they do know is that they have directed their action towards a specific piece of the puzzle.

That is why we speak of intervened anchors, not resolved anchors.

Examples:

  - «I think the creature depends on that crack; I try to close it.»

  - «I think it moves following a pattern; I position myself where I think it is going to move.»

  - «I think the fire feeds it; I try to cut off the path of the flames.»

  - «I think it only perceives sounds; I coordinate the group through gestures.»

  - «I think its fixation on me can be used to lure it away from the focus.»

In short, intervening an anchor is interpreting it. Whether or not the player resolves it will depend on the roll.

### **The confrontation dice pool**

When the players decide to face the confrontation, they build a single dice pool. The pool always starts from 1D6 as a base. That die is contributed by the character who takes the initiative of the main action. Then, 1D6 is added for each intervened anchor.

The unit that adds dice is not an action, but an intervened anchor. This means that one action may intervene a single anchor or several, if the narration justifies it.

For example: «I pour oil over the ground to reveal its footprints when it disappears and, at the same time, to drive it towards the lit area.»

This action could intervene two anchors if both are present in the scene: its invisibility or displacement, and its relationship with light or shadow.

In this way, each intervention will contribute 1D6 to the pool, provided the GD considers that its reading is sufficiently supported by the fiction.

### **Actions, anchors and execution**

A player may have several ideas, but cannot always carry them all out.

It may happen that a player interprets two different anchors and proposes two valid solutions, but that both require simultaneous actions that are incompatible for a single character. In that case, the actions can be shared out.

Example: A player says: «Two things need doing: someone must keep it away from the crack and someone must break the circle that feeds it.»

Although the interpretation may have come from a single player, each action must be carried out by a character within the fiction. Thus, one character may take charge of luring the creature, while another intervenes the circle. Each rolls the die or dice corresponding to the anchor they are intervening.

This allows tactical intelligence to be shared without erasing the physical and narrative presence of the other characters.

Understanding is not the same as acting. Every action needs a body in the scene.

### **Collaborative confrontations**

In a collaborative confrontation, several characters intervene different anchors to build a single common pool.

Each player rolls the dice corresponding to the anchors their character intervenes or the action they perform. The character who takes the initiative also rolls the base D6.

Although the dice are shared out among several players, they all form part of a single confrontation roll.

Example:

  - PC A takes the initiative and declares the objective: to expel the creature by closing its bond. In addition, they intervene the anchor of the threat.

  - PC A will roll 1D6 base and 1D6 for the intervened anchor.

  - PC B intervenes the movement anchor: they will roll 1D6.

  - PC C intervenes the anchor of the surroundings and of the bond: this adds 2D6. But they cannot perform both actions alone. So they will roll 1D6 for performing the first action, and PC D, who performs the second action, another D6.

The total pool is 5D6.

The dice may be physically shared out among the players, but the result is interpreted as a single joint action.

### **Individual confrontations**

A character may also decide to act alone.

In that case, they build their own pool:

  - 1D6 base

  - +1D6 for each anchor they themselves intervene.

This may be riskier, because they will have less narrative support and fewer dice. But it can also avoid collective exposure.

The system does not force anyone to cooperate. Cooperation is a possibility, not an imposition.

Two modes may even coexist in the same scene: a group of characters may coordinate a collaborative action while another character tries to resolve the confrontation on their own.

If both actions target the same contender, any valid success may transform or resolve the conflict.

### **The confrontation roll**

Once the pool has been built, the players taking part in the confrontation must declare, by consensus, their objective for that confrontation. Only then are the dice rolled. The roll is resolved with the usual rules for rolls followed by the _Numen_ system (successes and failures).

**Success**

If the roll succeeds, the declared objective is achieved.

This does not necessarily mean that the creature dies. In Numinia, entities do not die in a material sense. They are dissolved, contained, deactivated, unanchored…

The result must respect the objective declared by the players.

If the objective was «to close the crack that sustains the Echo Predator», a success may cause the creature to come undone and be dragged back into the Akasha.

If the objective was «to pin it down long enough to flee», a success does not eliminate the creature, but it allows escape.

**Total Success**

With three or more net successes, the result is especially favourable. The GD may grant additional effects.

**Failure without consequences**

If there are neither 6s nor 1s, the action fails to prevail, which indicates that the anchors the PCs were trying to intervene were not entirely correct ‒in this respect, it is worth pointing out that intervening an anchor is, generally speaking, a hypothesis on the PC's part, not a certainty‒, or that their skill in carrying it out was not sufficient.

The confrontation continues, but the players must reconsider their interpretation or take another path in the scene. That is to say, detect other anchors or think of another way of intervening those detected earlier.

**Critical Failure**

If there are failures and no successes, the confrontation is not resolved favourably, but neither is it repeated in the same way; the critical failure transforms the conflict.

The creature responds, the scene changes, the anchors are altered, a new pressure appears and, what is more, and this is not open to appeal, the characters acquire a state. A critical failure does not return the confrontation to the previous point. It turns it into another confrontation.

The State the PCs acquire is variable, at the GD's discretion. But if the Table of States in the following section is consulted, it can be seen that one of the states indicates, specifically, that the PC is _Unanchored_ for having been unable to intervene an anchor. Although this seems the ideal State to acquire in a confrontation, the GD, depending on the nature of the creature and the circumstances, may decide to impose a different state. Even a different state for each of the PCs involved.

### **Multiple interpretations**

It may happen that two characters interpret the same phenomenon differently.

For example:

  - One player believes the creature is anchored to the darkness

  - Another believes that what matters is that it avoids the light

The system does not require only one of them to be right. If both interpretations are coherent with the scene, both can be brought into play. If the roll succeeds, the GD integrates them as two angles of a single reality.

The resolution need not necessarily say: «One of you was right and the other was not.» Rather, it would be: «You were both reading different parts of the same structure.»

But let us remember that interpretation in Numinia, although multiple, is not arbitrary. One may interpret; one may not invent without grounds.

### **Operational summary**

  - The GD describes the contender and the scene.

  - A clear objective is defined for the action.

  - The players interpret possible anchors.

  - The players declare which anchors they intervene.

  - The pool is built: 1D6 base + 1D6 for each intervened anchor.

  - Each player rolls the dice corresponding to their interventions (or actions).

  - All the dice form a single roll.

  - If there is success, the objective is achieved.

  - If there is failure, the confrontation continues with other anchors or other interventions.

  - If there is critical failure, the conflict is transformed and generates states.

  - The confrontation continues only if the new situation demands it.

### **Final principle**

Confrontation in Numinia does not consist of striking until you win. It consists of reading a presence, interpreting its anchors, deciding how much is understood, taking on the risk and acting. Sometimes, intervening two or three anchors will be enough. Sometimes, the group will need to coordinate several actions. Sometimes a partial interpretation will suffice. At other times, a critical failure will transform the scene completely. The player is never obliged to solve the whole puzzle in order to act.

## **Fragment 6: PC States**

In the game of Numinia, the existence of the PCs is not measured in hit points or energy points, as in other role-playing games. Let us remember, to begin with, that all living beings in the universe of Numinia are something more than a material body. Even the Numinids are beings subject to a presence that moves between the physical world and the virtual spectrum. Their nature is subject to the Veil, to the same ethereal force of the Akasha that contains the city. Thus, the «death» of a PC does not happen as it does in our world; rather, it corresponds to a disappearance or disintegration of that «metaphysical» body which makes up the manifestation of that PC.

To represent any distortion or alteration ‒and even possible dissolution‒ that their bodily form suffers, the game develops a system of States, which are acquired in different situations and as a consequence of adverse circumstances.

States represent, then, the repercussions of badly resolved conflicts.

They are not physical damage. A character does not lose life because a creature has struck them. In Numinia, a character becomes Confused, Contaminated, Unanchored, Fragmented or Desynchronised because the reality of the conflict has interfered with their way of acting.

States affect the character's capacity to go on acting and intervening effectively. Each situation indicates which states it can induce when it responds to a critical failure or to an adverse position in the face of the conflict ‒without a Critical Failure result on a dice roll being necessary‒.

For example, an Echo Predator could induce: _Desynchronised_, because it alters the character's rhythm of action; _Contaminated_, because it leaves putrid residues of the Akasha in their presence. But mere exposure to a toxic substance could induce the same Contaminated state, while some kind of cognitive dissonance could induce a _Confused_ state.

### **Progression of States**

A PC can only have one active State. They begin in the Unscathed State, which is the same as having no State. If an Unscathed PC acquires one, they fall under that new State.

If a character who already has a State acquires another, they do not accumulate both: they pass directly to the _Collapsed_ State. This is, moreover, the only way to acquire that State. If a _Collapsed_ character acquires another State again, they pass to the _Lethargic_ State. Likewise, this is the only way to acquire that State.

A _Lethargic_ character is left out of the immediate action. They are not dissolved, but they are inactive and, therefore, very vulnerable. They may be recovered if the fiction allows it and other characters intervene. If a _Lethargic_ character suffers a conclusive consequence once again, they may become _Dissolved_. This represents a farewell in the world of Numinia. It is the GD who will decide at what moment and how this last State is acquired.

### **Healing States**

Nevertheless, there are different ways to eliminate a State and return to Unscathed. In the first place, a long day of continuous rest (24 hours) can eliminate any State, if the GD sees fit, except for the _Collapsed_ - _Lethargic_ States. These last two will only be recovered at the end of the campaign, as their elimination is reckoned to require some three or four weeks.

There are, on the other hand, some objects that can eliminate a specific state, which are accounted for in Chapter 6, _Inventory and Bestiary_. But they are not objects that can be found easily; rather, they will be available to the extent the GD considers appropriate. In general, they can be found on the black market of the Ouroboros District, but always in exchange for other objects or goods, since money has little relevance in the world of Numinia.

Lastly, the most direct and simple way to eliminate a State is the Prism Cells <sup>13</sup>. Prism Cells are, like the Seeds of Knowledge, symbolic units that reflect the way in which a player manifests themselves in the world of Numinia.

Prism Cells are natural treasures, which may be found by chance anywhere in Numinia ‒they proliferate more on the periphery‒. There are four groups of Prism Cells, each one related to a Faction. Thus, a PC may collect Prism Cells of whatever type they wish, but may only use those belonging to their own Faction.

Prism Cells have several practical uses in the game; the most advantageous is eliminating a State. However, each State has a different cost in cells.

**Cost in Prism Cells**

Needless to say, the cells used are spent; they are single-use tokens. The mechanic is automatic: the PC digests the required cells and in some five minutes the State disappears. It is understood, however, that there are extreme situations in which it is difficult to take out the necessary cells and bring them to one's mouth to swallow them.
